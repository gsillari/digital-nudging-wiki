Gray et al., 2018: The Dark (Patterns) Side of UX Design

Summary

The Dark (Patterns) Side of UX Design is an early HCI source for treating Dark Patterns as a design-ethics problem rather than merely as scattered interface tricks. The paper analyzes practitioner-identified dark patterns and argues that they arise when human-centered design values are co-opted to serve deceptive or exploitative organizational goals.

The paper is especially useful for the course because it makes the normative reversal vivid. Classical nudging and Choice Architecture are defended when they are user-serving and choice-preserving. Dark patterns use the same design sensitivity to attention, salience, defaults, friction, and context, but redirect it toward extraction or control.

Key claims

  • Dark patterns are not simply bad usability. Many are effective designs according to conversion, retention, or shareholder metrics, while being hostile to user welfare.
  • The user-centered ideal of UX design can be displaced by business incentives when design expertise is used to make the user act against their own interests.
  • Dark patterns undermine users’ perceived action possibilities and communicative expectations: the interface withholds, disguises, or distorts the cues users need to understand what is happening.
  • Ethical diagnosis should not stop at whether a user technically had a choice. It should ask whether the interface made relevant choices visible, intelligible, and practically available.

Taxonomy

The paper organizes dark patterns into five high-level strategies:

  • Nagging: repeated interruption or redirection away from the user’s intended task.
  • Obstruction: making an action harder than it needs to be, including roach motel patterns, price comparison prevention, and intermediate currencies.
  • Sneaking: hiding, delaying, or disguising relevant information, including hidden costs, forced continuity, sneak into basket, and bait and switch.
  • Interface interference: manipulating visual hierarchy, discoverability, defaults, wording, or presentation so one option becomes easier to select or harder to notice.
  • Forced action: requiring the user to perform an action to access functionality, including social pyramid schemes, privacy-invasive disclosure, and gamified compulsion.

Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This source gives the vault a bridge from Choice Architecture to adversarial choice architecture (Dark Patterns). It shows how interface design can preserve formal choice while distorting the user’s practical ability to understand, refuse, compare, or exit.

For the course, the important point is that dark patterns are not merely the opposite of “nice” nudges. They are choice architectures whose objective has changed. The behavioral and interface mechanisms may be familiar from benign nudging, but the paternalistic constraint has been removed: the design serves the architect rather than the chooser.
