# Nudge - Introduction

## Place in the work

The introduction establishes the book's central vocabulary: [[Choice Architecture]], [[Nudge|nudges]], libertarian paternalism, Humans and Econs, and the claim that no decision environment is neutral. For the course, this is the primary unit for explaining what a nudge is before moving into digital settings.

## Summary

The chapter begins with the cafeteria example: food choices change when the same foods are arranged differently. The example matters because the cafeteria director cannot avoid arranging the food somehow. The resulting choice environment can be random, self-serving, profit-maximizing, or intentionally welfare-oriented, but it cannot be undesigned.

Thaler and Sunstein call the person who organizes the decision context a choice architect. Their examples already point toward digital nudging: benefit websites, forms, ballots, product shelves, medical explanations, and sales scripts all structure what people notice, understand, and do.

The introduction then defends libertarian paternalism. It is libertarian because people should remain free to choose and to opt out. It is paternalistic because the architect may legitimately try to help people make choices that improve their lives by their own standards. The chapter's definition of a nudge turns on four criteria: it is part of the choice architecture; it changes behavior predictably; it does not forbid options or substantially alter economic incentives; and it is easy and cheap to avoid.

The chapter closes by contrasting Econs and Humans. Econs respond mainly to incentives and choose well under standard economic assumptions. Humans also respond to incentives, but their choices are shaped by attention, framing, default effects, self-control, limited information, and social context. This makes nudges useful, especially where choice is complex or feedback is weak.

## Key claims

- Choice architecture is inevitable because options must be organized, displayed, sequenced, defaulted, explained, or withheld in some way.
- The claim of neutrality is often misleading: random design, active choice, and "let people choose" are themselves design choices.
- A nudge preserves choice and differs from bans, mandates, fines, taxes, subsidies, and other strong incentive changes.
- The authors' welfare test is chooser-centered: the goal is to help people make the choices they would make with fuller attention, information, cognitive capacity, and self-control.
- [[Defaults]] are a paradigmatic nudge because doing nothing often carries an implicit recommendation and a low-effort path.
- The same architecture can help or exploit. The introduction already flags [[Sludge]] as a private and public sector risk.

## Evidence or method

The chapter uses stylized examples and behavioral evidence rather than a single empirical study. The cafeteria example shows salience and arrangement effects. The Schiphol urinal target illustrates attention steering through a tiny visual cue. Employee benefits and smartphone settings illustrate default effects. Health, retirement, and risk behavior show why the Econ model is often too demanding for real-world design.

For this course, only a few examples need to be retained: cafeteria layout for nonneutral design, fruit at eye level for the nudge definition, defaults for inertia, and benefit websites/forms as an early bridge to digital choice architecture.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital nudging starts from this chapter's core proposition: every interface is a choice architecture. Sign-up flows, privacy settings, consent banners, recommender rankings, notification systems, pricing pages, account cancellation screens, and app defaults all organize choice. The introduction gives students the distinction they need throughout the course: a helpful digital nudge preserves meaningful exit and helps users by their own lights; a manipulative design or high-friction opt-out is closer to [[Sludge]].
