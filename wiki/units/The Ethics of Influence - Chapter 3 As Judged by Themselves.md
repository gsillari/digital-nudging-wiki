# The Ethics of Influence - Chapter 3 As Judged by Themselves

## Core idea

Chapter 3 develops the welfare criterion behind [[Ethics of Nudging]]: a nudge should generally make choosers better off as judged by themselves. This is not the same as satisfying whatever people happen to choose in a poorly informed, impulsive, or manipulated moment. It asks what people would endorse under better conditions of information, attention, reflection, and self-control.

The chapter is the main source for [[Chooser Welfare]] in the vault.

## Key claims

- Actual choices do not always reveal welfare. People may lack information, misunderstand consequences, face complexity, or act against their own reflective judgments.
- Choice architects should be cautious when claiming to know what choosers would want. The standard is chooser-centered, but the architect can still smuggle in outside values.
- Self-control problems matter because people may have reflective commitments that conflict with momentary impulses. A good nudge may support the reflective commitment without eliminating choice.
- Second-order preferences matter. People often have preferences about their preferences, such as wanting to want healthier food, less social media, or more saving.
- Ex ante and ex post judgments can diverge. A nudge may shape the preference it later appears to satisfy, which creates a circularity problem.
- Data, pilots, experiments, opt-out rates, and direct welfare inquiry can help, but they do not remove the need for normative judgment.

## Tensions

The chapter is valuable because it refuses an easy answer. Revealed preference is too thin, but expert paternalism is too arrogant. For digital nudging, that tension is central: click behavior, engagement, retention, and conversion are easy to measure, but they may not track the user's own reflective welfare.

The chapter also creates a hard problem for personalization. A system may infer what a user wants from behavior, but the behavior may itself be produced by addictive design, social pressure, poor defaults, or incomplete information. The more a platform shapes the environment, the less cleanly observed behavior can function as evidence of welfare.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital systems often treat revealed behavior as the user's preference: clicks, dwell time, purchases, scroll depth, return frequency, and completion rates. Chapter 3 gives the course a reason to distrust that shortcut. A digital nudge is ethically stronger when it can connect behavior change to user-endorsed goals, not merely to observed compliance.

This chapter should be used whenever the course asks what counts as a user-serving digital intervention. It supports questions such as: would users endorse the design if they understood the data used, the objective optimized, the alternatives suppressed, and the long-term consequences?
