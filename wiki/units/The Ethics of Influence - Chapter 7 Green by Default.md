# The Ethics of Influence - Chapter 7 Green by Default

## Core idea

Chapter 7 applies the ethics framework to default rules in environmental policy. The central example is a green default: a system can make environmentally preferable options the default while allowing people to opt out. The chapter is useful beyond environmental policy because it shows how defaults, active choosing, and personalization each carry different ethical risks.

## Key claims

- Defaults can be justified by externalities, bounded rationality, and welfare gains, but their legitimacy depends on transparency, ease of opt-out, and the fit between the default and chooser interests.
- Active choosing can protect against self-serving default setters and can be better for contested or value-laden choices, but it imposes attention and decision costs.
- Influenced active choice can prompt people to decide while also making some considerations salient.
- Personalized defaults may improve fit, but they raise questions about data, prediction, privacy, error, accountability, and institutional incentives.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital environments are default-rich. Privacy settings, notification settings, recommendation feeds, payment options, sharing controls, renewal terms, security features, cookie permissions, and app permissions all require a rule for what happens if the user does nothing.

Chapter 7 gives the course a way to evaluate these rules without assuming that active choice is always best. A well-chosen digital default may reduce burden and protect users. But a hidden or sticky default can become a vehicle for data extraction, lock-in, unwanted sharing, or sustained attention capture.

The personalization discussion is especially relevant to digital nudging. Personalized defaults are attractive because software can tailor them to user history and context. They are also risky because the platform may know much more about the prediction rule than the user, and because the default may serve a proprietary objective rather than the user's own judgment.
