# Coglianese and Lehr, 2019: Transparency and Algorithmic Governance

## Summary

[[Coglianese and Lehr, 2019|Transparency and Algorithmic Governance]] distinguishes two forms of transparency. Fishbowl transparency concerns access to information: data, records, code, meetings, documents, and evidence about what government is doing. [[Reasoned Transparency]] concerns why a decision was made: the purpose, justification, reasoning, and validation that make an action intelligible and contestable.

Coglianese and Lehr argue that machine learning's black-box character does not automatically make algorithmic governance legally impermissible. Administrative law is pragmatic about transparency. It often requires adequate reasons, not total exposure of every internal mechanism. Agencies can often satisfy reason-giving obligations by explaining the objective function, design choices, basic operation, validation efforts, and institutional role of the algorithm.

## Key claims

- "Transparency" is not one thing. Fishbowl transparency and reasoned transparency do different normative and legal work.
- Black-box models create a challenge for reason-giving, but not an automatic legal barrier.
- Legal transparency requirements are pragmatic and context-sensitive rather than absolute.
- Full source-code access may be neither necessary nor sufficient for accountability.
- Agencies should explain the system's objective, data sources, design choices, validation, accuracy, limitations, and decision role.
- Reasoned transparency can sometimes be supported by partial fishbowl transparency, auditing, documentation, and validation.
- Advances in explainable AI may improve the fit between machine learning and administrative reason-giving, but governance cannot wait for perfect interpretability.

## Course use

Use this paper to refine the digital-nudging transparency module. It prevents "transparency" from becoming a vague moral word. A platform can disclose that it uses personalization without giving useful reasons; conversely, a system can be partly opaque internally while still giving a meaningful public account of its goal, evidence, validation, and safeguards.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital nudging raises the same distinction. Users, researchers, and regulators may need different kinds of transparency. A user may need to know why an option was recommended; a regulator may need access to experiments and objective functions; a researcher may need audit data; the public may need a reasoned account of why the system is legitimate.

This is especially important when proprietary systems claim trade secrecy. The paper supports the course's layered view: full code disclosure is not the only path to accountability, but reasoned explanation, validation, and auditability are nonoptional.
