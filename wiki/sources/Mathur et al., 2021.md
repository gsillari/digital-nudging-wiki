# Mathur et al., 2021: What Makes a Dark Pattern... Dark?

## Summary

[[Mathur et al., 2021|What Makes a Dark Pattern... Dark?]] asks what exactly makes a dark pattern normatively objectionable. The paper argues that the field needs more than lists of examples. It needs a way to connect observable design attributes to the normative reason a design is troubling and to the method used to measure harm.

This makes the paper a systematic complement to the earlier empirical and taxonomy work. It clarifies the difference between description, evaluation, and measurement.

## Key claims

- Dark patterns modify the decision space and manipulate information flow.
- Descriptive attributes include asymmetry, restrictiveness, disparate treatment, covertness, deception, and information hiding.
- No single normative lens captures all dark-pattern concerns.
- Measurement should match the normative concern being invoked. A study of financial loss, privacy invasion, consumer autonomy, competition, or legal compliance will need different evidence.
- Dark patterns should be understood in relation to nudges, sludge, manipulation, and market manipulation rather than as a purely HCI category.

## Normative lenses

The paper organizes evaluation around four lenses:

- Individual welfare: financial loss, privacy invasion, cognitive burden, wasted time, and frustrated preference satisfaction.
- Collective welfare: harms to competition, marketplace trust, and the distribution of burdens across populations.
- Regulatory objectives: compliance with consumer-protection, privacy, and data-protection regimes such as the FTC Act, GDPR, CPRA, and related rules.
- Individual autonomy: whether the design impairs authorship over one's own decision by hiding alternatives, exploiting inattention, or making refusal harder to exercise.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This source gives the course a way to avoid treating "dark" as a vibe. It asks what value has been impaired and what evidence would show the impairment.

That is especially useful when the course moves from classic nudging to digital nudging. A design can be user-serving under one lens and troubling under another: it may reduce effort while increasing privacy loss, or increase conversion while reducing autonomy. Mathur et al. help separate the mechanical question "what does the interface do?" from the normative question "why is that bad?" and the empirical question "how would we know?"
