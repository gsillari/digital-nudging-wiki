# Behavioral Economics

## Core idea

[[Behavioral Economics]] studies how real people make judgments and decisions when attention, information, cognitive capacity, emotion, self-control, social influence, and institutional design depart from the assumptions of the Econ model. In [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]], it supplies the reason [[Nudge|nudges]] can work: Humans make predictable mistakes in predictable environments.

## Key distinctions

- Econ vs Human: Econs optimize under standard assumptions; Humans use shortcuts, forget, follow defaults, respond to frames, and care about social cues.
- Fallibility vs irrationality: Thaler and Sunstein avoid treating people as foolish. Their claim is that people are fallible and environments can make decisions hard.
- Judgment biases vs self-control problems: anchoring, availability, representativeness, loss aversion, and framing differ from hot-cold states, temptation, and Planner/Doer conflict.
- Individual cognition vs social influence: people are shaped both by internal shortcuts and by what others appear to think or do.
- Education vs design: boosting competence can help, but the book argues that good choice architecture remains necessary because many decisions are too complex, rare, or poorly timed for education alone.

## Evidence and debate

[[Nudge - Part I Humans and Econs]] maps the book's behavioral economics foundation. Chapter 1 covers heuristics, biases, framing, and Automatic/Reflective systems. Chapter 2 covers self-control, temptation, commitment devices, and mental accounting. Chapter 3 covers conformity, social norms, cascades, identity, and pluralistic ignorance.

[[EAST Framework]] shows the applied side of this tradition: behavioral regularities can be converted into intervention properties such as ease, salience, social relevance, and timing, but they still require contextual testing.

[[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]] reframes the field's next step. If behavioral economics identifies mistakes, algorithmic behavioral economics asks whether tools can help people correct mistakes while respecting their heterogeneous preferences.

The debate for the course is not whether every bias always appears. It is whether a digital environment activates predictable vulnerabilities, and whether design can responsibly reduce error without becoming manipulative or paternalistic in an objectionable way.

## Practical or policy relevance

Digital environments are rich behavioral environments. They can anchor users with suggested values, exploit inertia through autoplay or renewal, make social proof salient, hide costs, overwhelm attention, or help users by simplifying and timing decisions better. Behavioral economics gives the course its mechanism vocabulary.

Behavioral Economics 2.0 adds a tool-building vocabulary: algorithms can explain, predict, warn, implement, protect, and train, not only nudge.
