# Dark Pattern Taxonomy

## Core idea

[[Dark Pattern Taxonomy]] organizes recurring manipulative interface strategies so that researchers, teachers, regulators, designers, and auditors can identify them without treating every bad interface as unique.

The taxonomy problem has two parts. First, examples need names: hard to cancel, sneak into basket, confirmshaming, hidden costs, bad defaults, countdown timers. Second, those examples need structure: what higher-level strategy do they implement, and what normative harm do they create?

## From early taxonomy to ontology

[[Gray et al., 2018|The Dark (Patterns) Side of UX Design]] organizes practitioner-identified patterns into five strategies: nagging, obstruction, sneaking, interface interference, and forced action.

[[Mathur et al., 2019|Dark Patterns at Scale]] gives an empirical e-commerce taxonomy with seven categories: sneaking, urgency, misdirection, social proof, scarcity, obstruction, and forced action. Its 15 types include hidden costs, hidden subscription, countdown timer, confirmshaming, trick questions, hard to cancel, and forced enrollment.

[[Mathur et al., 2021|What Makes a Dark Pattern... Dark?]] adds attributes that help evaluate patterns: asymmetry, restrictiveness, disparate treatment, covertness, deception, and information hiding.

[[Gray et al., 2024|An Ontology of Dark Patterns Knowledge]] harmonizes academic and regulatory taxonomies into a three-level ontology:

- High-level patterns: broad strategies such as obstruction, sneaking, interface interference, forced action, and social engineering.
- Meso-level patterns: middle-level mechanisms or angles of attack.
- Low-level patterns: situated interface executions that can be detected in concrete designs.

## Why levels matter

Different levels answer different questions. A low-level label such as "countdown timer" helps identify a design. A meso-level category such as "urgency" explains the mechanism. A high-level category such as "social engineering" connects the mechanism to a broader strategy of steering through social and emotional pressure.

This matters for regulation and teaching because a list of examples can become obsolete quickly. A levelled ontology makes it easier to classify new digital interfaces, including AI assistants, recommender systems, consent banners, subscription flows, and gamified apps.

## Course use

For the course, examples should be used to fix the concept, not to exhaust it. Students should learn to ask:

- Is this pattern hiding information, creating friction, manipulating presentation, forcing disclosure, or applying social pressure?
- Is the problem deception, autonomy impairment, welfare loss, privacy loss, competition harm, or legal noncompliance?
- Is the pattern static, or is it being tested and personalized through [[Continuous Optimization and Experimentation]]?
