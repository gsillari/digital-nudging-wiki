# Behavioral Economics 2.0

## Core idea

[[Behavioral Economics 2.0]] is Ludwig, Mullainathan, Pink, and Rambachan's programmatic name for a behavioral economics built around algorithmic tools rather than only behavioral interventions. The goal is to help people make decisions closer to [[Reflective Equilibrium]], not merely to move behavior in a direction assumed to be good on average.

The concept is a positive-potential counterpart to [[Hypernudge]]. It treats AI and algorithms as possible instruments of agency, deliberation, debiasing, and welfare improvement.

## Paradigm shift

- Behavioral Economics 1.0: identifies systematic mistakes and designs nudges, defaults, simplifications, and reminders to improve behavior on average.
- Precision nudging: uses machine learning to target the right nudge, user, timing, or format for a specified behavioral direction.
- Behavioral Economics 2.0: asks which direction is right for this person and designs algorithms that help them get closer to that choice.

The shift is from intervention design to tool building. A nudge is done to someone; an algorithmic thought partner is something a person can use.

[[Mills, 2022|Personalized Nudging (Mills)]] is the systematic complement to this programmatic shift. Mills distinguishes delivery personalization from content or target personalization, while Behavioral Economics 2.0 pushes the target question further toward [[Reflective Equilibrium]].

## Core capacities

- Explain confusing options and instructions.
- Fill informational blind spots with prediction or external knowledge.
- Highlight psychological traps such as sunk costs, projection bias, or gambler's fallacy.
- Help implement chosen actions.
- Protect users in adversarial markets.
- Train better judgment through repeated practice and feedback.

## Digital relevance

Behavioral Economics 2.0 broadens [[Digital Nudging]] beyond interface steering. It includes decision aids, AI agents, explanatory systems, debiasing trainers, adaptive consumer protection tools, and human-AI deliberation environments.

The ethical promise is that digital systems might preserve agency better than classic nudges by helping people reason toward their own considered judgments. The risk is that the same systems could smuggle in external objectives, overfit to observed behavior, or become paternalistic under the language of assistance.
