# Nudge

## Core idea

A [[Nudge]] is a behaviorally informed change to [[Choice Architecture]] that predictably steers behavior while preserving meaningful freedom to choose otherwise. In [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]], the core tests are that options remain available, economic incentives are not substantially changed, and avoidance is easy and cheap.

For a course-level synthesis of the concept and its ethical constraints, see [[Nudging and the Ethics of Nudging]].

For digital nudging, the concept applies to interface details such as defaults, reminders, prompts, ordering, salience, warnings, feedback, friction, recommendation structures, and simplified forms.

[[Hansen and Jespersen, 2013|Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice]] sharpens the definition by emphasizing intentionality: a nudge is best treated as an intentional attempt to influence behavior, rather than any contextual feature that happens to influence behavior. This matters because responsibility attaches to the architect once influence is deliberate.

[[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge: Big Data as a Mode of Regulation by Design]] marks the digital extension of the concept. A [[Hypernudge]] is not merely a nudge delivered online; it is data-driven, personalized, dynamic, and continuously updated through feedback.

[[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]] pushes beyond nudging by asking when algorithms can become tools for thought partnership rather than interventions that move people toward a predetermined target.

## Key distinctions

- Nudge vs mandate: a mandate requires or forbids action; a nudge preserves the option to go another way.
- Nudge vs incentive: incentives change payoffs; nudges often work by changing attention, effort, salience, framing, or default status.
- Nudge vs [[Sludge]]: a nudge should help users by their own lights; sludge uses friction to block or burden welfare-enhancing action.
- Nudge vs boost: a boost improves competence or capability; a nudge changes the choice environment. Thaler and Sunstein argue that the two can and should often be combined.
- Transparent nudge vs [[Manipulation]]: ethical nudging should be visible, publicly defensible, and compatible with reflective choice.
- Type 1 vs type 2 nudge: [[Nudge Typology]] distinguishes nudges that influence automatic behavior from nudges that shape reflective choice.
- Static nudge vs [[Hypernudge]]: static nudges arrange a relatively stable choice environment; hypernudges continuously reconfigure informational choice architecture using data and algorithms.
- Nudge vs [[Algorithmic Thought Partners|algorithmic thought partner]]: a nudge changes the environment of choice; a thought partner helps the user understand and reason about the choice.

## Evidence and debate

[[Nudge - Introduction]] gives the foundational definition and examples. [[Nudge - Part I Humans and Econs]] explains why people are responsive to nudges: heuristics, self-control problems, default effects, framing, and social influence. [[Nudge - Chapter 4 When Do We Need a Nudge]] identifies conditions under which nudges are most useful. [[Nudge - Chapter 5 Choice Architecture]] gives the main design toolkit. [[EAST Framework]] gives a practitioner shorthand for many nudges and related behavioral interventions: Easy, Attractive, Social, and Timely.

[[Sunstein, 2016|The Ethics of Influence: Government in the Age of Behavioral Science]] gives the concept its ethical framework: nudges should be evaluated through welfare, autonomy, dignity, and self-government. The book's "as judged by themselves" standard is captured here as [[Chooser Welfare]].

[[Hansen and Jespersen, 2013|Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice]] adds a second debate: formal freedom to choose otherwise may not be enough, because nudges are used precisely where people are predictably fallible. The practical question is whether users can notice, understand, and resist the nudge in context.

[[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge: Big Data as a Mode of Regulation by Design]] adds the digital debate: when nudges are personalized, dynamic, and opaque, classical tests such as opt-out, transparency, and consent become harder to apply.

[[Behavioral Economics 2.0]] adds the paradigm debate: perhaps digital nudging should not only ask how to make nudges more precise, but how to build tools that help users reach their own considered judgments.

The main debates concern welfare, autonomy, transparency, manipulation, and sufficiency. A nudge may be too weak for externalities or serious harms; a nudge may become manipulative if the user cannot understand or easily avoid it; and a nudge may serve the architect rather than the chooser.

## Practical or policy relevance

In digital systems, nudges can improve welfare by helping users complete beneficial tasks, avoid errors, compare options, remember intentions, understand consequences, and resist harmful defaults. They can also be used for platform-serving goals such as retention, engagement, data capture, or upselling. The course should therefore ask not only "does it change behavior?" but "whose welfare does the design optimize, and how easy is it to resist?"
