Coglianese and Crum, 2025b: Regulating Multifunctionality

Summary

Regulating Multifunctionality argues that foundation models and generative AI intensify the basic problem of AI heterogeneity. A foundation model is not a single-purpose tool with one predictable risk profile. It is closer to a Swiss Army knife: the same underlying system can be adapted to many tasks, domains, users, and harms.

That multifunctionality makes prescriptive, one-size-fits-all regulation especially weak. A rule designed for one use can underreach, overreach, or become obsolete when the same model is used differently. The authors argue that even more flexible tools such as performance standards and ex post liability can struggle because regulators may not be able to monitor all uses or trace harms easily. Their preferred response is Management-Based AI Regulation: require developers and users to build internal risk-management practices that can adapt to the tool's changing uses.

Key claims

  • AI heterogeneity varies across use, design, and problem contexts.
  • Foundation models increase heterogeneity because one model can support many downstream functions.
  • Multifunctionality makes fixed design standards and broad prohibitions difficult to calibrate.
  • Performance standards can fail when performance is hard to define across many uses.
  • Disclosure and liability can help, but they often face monitoring, information, and enforcement limits.
  • Management-based regulation is better suited to multifunctional AI because it shifts attention to proactive risk identification, assessment, documentation, mitigation, and revision.
  • Regulators will need vigilance, agility, technical capacity, leadership, resources, and a culture of regulatory excellence.

Course use

Use this paper to name why digital nudging is not governed well by simple lists of allowed and forbidden interface features. The same recommender, assistant, or personalization engine can inform, persuade, exploit, exclude, explain, or distract depending on its context and objective.

The paper also helps explain why foundation models complicate the course's later material. LLMs can become choice architects, advisors, tutors, sales agents, compliance tools, emotional companions, and decision aids. The behavioral and ethical risk is not inherent in one function but emerges from the use-case ecology.

Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital nudging increasingly depends on multifunctional AI systems. A chatbot can be a disclosure interface in one context, a persuasive sales assistant in another, and a bureaucratic gatekeeper in a third. That makes the ethical evaluation of digital nudging more context-sensitive and more organizational.

For the course, this paper supports a governance thesis: when digital choice architecture is multifunctional, the central regulatory object is not only the interface or the model, but the risk-management system that decides how the model is used.
