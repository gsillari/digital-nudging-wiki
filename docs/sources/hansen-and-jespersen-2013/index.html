Hansen and Jespersen, 2013: Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice

Summary

Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice argues that the usual debate over nudging is too blunt. Critics often say nudges manipulate choice; defenders often reply that choice architecture is unavoidable, nudges preserve liberty, and publicity is enough. Hansen and Jespersen reject both shortcuts.

Their core contribution is a framework for distinguishing nudges by two dimensions: whether they target automatic behavior or reflective choice, and whether the intention and means are epistemically transparent to the person being nudged. This produces the Nudge Typology used in this vault.

Key claims

  • A nudge should be understood as an intentional attempt to influence behavior, not merely as any contextual feature that happens to affect behavior.
  • The fact that contexts always influence people does not dissolve responsibility. Intentional intervention adds agency, ends, and accountability.
  • Formal freedom to choose otherwise is not enough. Nudges are used precisely because people often do not act like hyper-rational agents, so practical avoidability matters.
  • Libertarian paternalism and the nudge approach are distinct. Nudging is a means; libertarian paternalism is a constraint on ends.
  • Nudges are not always manipulations of choice. Some influence automatic behavior without engaging reflective choice.
  • Rawls-style publicity is not enough as an ethics test. A policy may be publicly defensible and still fail to be transparent to the person being nudged in the moment of intervention.
  • Epistemic transparency matters: a nudge is transparent when the person nudged can reasonably reconstruct the intention behind it and the means by which behavior change is pursued.

The typology

The paper distinguishes four types of nudges:

  • Transparent type 2 nudges: influence reflective choice in a way that makes intention and means apparent. These are close to empowerment nudges because they prompt consistent, reflective choice.
  • Transparent type 1 nudges: influence automatic behavior while making intention and means apparent. These may be acceptable, but they are not fully libertarian because the initial effect may be hard to avoid.
  • Non-transparent type 1 nudges: influence automatic behavior under the radar. These manipulate behavior rather than choice and require stronger disclosure, democratic grounding, and responsibility for effects and side effects.
  • Non-transparent type 2 nudges: influence reflective choice without epistemic transparency. These are the clearest cases of manipulation of choice and should generally be avoided without explicit individual consent.

Tensions

The paper is important because it preserves the force of both sides of the debate. Defenders are right that not every nudge is manipulative, but critics are right that intentional behavioral steering cannot hide behind the inevitability of choice architecture.

The paper also complicates the idea that transparency is simple. Publicly explaining a policy somewhere is different from designing an intervention so that the target can recognize the intention and mechanism while being influenced.

Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital nudging makes Hansen and Jespersen's framework highly reusable. Digital systems can target automatic behavior through layout, color, defaults, scrolling, timing, notifications, friction, and habit loops. They can also target reflective choice through framing, rankings, comparisons, warnings, personalization, social proof, and scarcity cues.

The typology helps the course ask what exactly a digital intervention is doing:

  • Is it influencing behavior without reflection, or shaping the premises of reflection?
  • Can the user reconstruct the platform's intention and mechanism?
  • Is formal opt-out meaningful in practice, or does the nudge work because users cannot realistically notice and resist it?
  • Who is responsible for side effects when automated experiments discover non-transparent influence?

This is also where proprietary digital systems become ethically murky. A platform may disclose its general policy while hiding the operative mechanism in ranking models, A/B tests, personalization rules, or interface variants. Hansen and Jespersen's epistemic transparency criterion therefore pushes the course beyond checkbox disclosure toward usable transparency and accountability.
