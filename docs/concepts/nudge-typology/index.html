Nudge Typology

Core idea

Nudge Typology classifies nudges by two questions: what cognitive process the intervention targets, and whether the person being nudged can recognize the intention and mechanism of influence. The typology comes from Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice and is useful because it avoids treating all nudges as either harmless or manipulative.

Dimensions

  • Type 1 vs type 2: Type 1 nudges influence automatic behavior without engaging reflective choice. Type 2 nudges influence the attention, framing, or premises of reflective choice.
  • Transparent vs non-transparent: a transparent nudge lets the target reasonably reconstruct the intention behind the intervention and the means by which behavior change is pursued. A non-transparent nudge does not.

Four types

  • Transparent type 2: prompting reflected choice. Examples include salient prompts, feedback, prompted choice, commitment devices, and consequence displays. These are the easiest to defend when they serve Chooser Welfare.
  • Transparent type 1: transparent influence of automatic behavior. Examples include visible visual illusions, alarms, or environmental cues whose purpose can be recognized. These can still be hard to avoid at first exposure.
  • Non-transparent type 1: manipulation of behavior. Examples include hidden changes to plate size, layout, background defaults, or other features that alter automatic behavior without recognition.
  • Non-transparent type 2: manipulation of choice. Examples include subtle risk framing, priming, anchoring, scarcity cues, or goal substitution that shape reflective choice without epistemic transparency.

Ethical significance

The typology adds nuance to Ethics of Nudging. Transparent type 2 nudges are close to empowerment because they support reflective choice and practical resistance. Transparent type 1 nudges require responsibility for effects because users may not be able to avoid the initial automatic response. Non-transparent type 1 nudges need stronger disclosure, consent, and accountability because responsibility for behavioral effects shifts toward the architect. Non-transparent type 2 nudges are the most ethically troubling because they manipulate choice while leaving responsibility with the chooser.

Digital relevance

Digital systems can instantiate all four types. A just-in-time warning can be transparent type 2. A visible progress cue or alert tone can be transparent type 1. A hidden default, ranking rule, or layout experiment can be non-transparent type 1. A personalized scarcity message, subtly framed consent request, or targeted social proof can be non-transparent type 2.

The typology is therefore a practical diagnostic tool for digital nudging: identify the target process, inspect transparency, ask who bears responsibility, and decide what level of consent, disclosure, audit, or prohibition is appropriate.

Hypernudge complicates the typology because digital interventions can adapt over time. A nudge may be transparent to some users, non-transparent to others, or become less transparent as personalization and algorithmic ranking grow more complex.
