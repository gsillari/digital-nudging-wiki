Nudge - Chapter 5 Choice Architecture

Place in the work

Chapter 5 is the main design toolkit chapter. It takes the claim that Humans are nudge-able and translates it into principles for designing environments, systems, forms, products, and interfaces.

Summary

The chapter begins with bad physical design: doors that signal "pull" when they must be pushed, stove controls whose layout does not map clearly onto burners, and other violations of stimulus-response compatibility. The lesson carries into choice design: if the system's signals conflict with the desired action, people will make predictable mistakes.

The chapter's central design mantra is to make desired action easy. Small "channel factors" can produce large changes when they remove a practical barrier between intention and action. In the tetanus-shot example, adding a map and plan prompt made action much more likely than information alone.

The chapter then develops key tools: Defaults, active choosing, prompted choice, error-forgiving design, feedback, better mappings from options to outcomes, structure for complex choices, curation, collaborative filtering, and attention to incentives and salience. It treats choice architecture as both behavioral and economic: designers should know who chooses, who uses, who pays, and who profits, and whether relevant incentives are visible to choosers.

Key claims

  • Good choice architecture aligns design signals with likely user action; bad architecture creates predictable errors.
  • "Make it easy" is the most portable design principle in the book.
  • Defaults are unavoidable and powerful, but they are not always sticky; people override them when outcomes are saliently bad or preferences are clear.
  • Active choice can protect consent and reveal preferences, but it can also be burdensome when decisions are complex.
  • Prompted choice is a softer alternative: users are asked to consider an option, while a default governs what happens if they proceed.
  • Systems should expect error and be forgiving: forcing functions, reminders, constraints, and error-proofing can prevent predictable mistakes.
  • Feedback helps people learn, but warning systems can fail if they become too frequent or easy to ignore.
  • Mapping improves choice by translating technical features into welfare-relevant consequences.
  • Complex choice sets require structure, curation, search, filtering, and sometimes recommendations.
  • Incentives matter, but behavioral design must ask whether incentives are actually salient.

Evidence or method

The chapter uses design examples from doors, stoves, subway tickets, parking garages, cars, medicine adherence, ATM cards, Gmail attachment reminders, London pedestrian warnings, battery alerts, ceiling paint, medical decisions, paint stores, online recommendations, and retirement tax incentives.

For the Digital Nudging course, the highest-yield examples are Gmail's missing-attachment prompt, default settings in software setup, online prompted choice, recommender systems as choice architecture, warning fatigue, and collaborative filtering as structured digital choice.

Relation to other units

This chapter operationalizes Nudge - Introduction and Nudge - Chapter 4 When Do We Need a Nudge. It also points forward to Nudge - Chapter 7 Smart Disclosure, which extends mapping and structured choice through machine-readable data, and to Nudge - Chapter 8 Sludge, which examines the harmful use of friction.

Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Most digital nudging is choice architecture in this chapter's sense. Interfaces choose defaults, sort options, highlight actions, schedule prompts, issue warnings, simplify or complicate tasks, personalize recommendations, and translate data into user-facing meanings. The chapter's toolkit can become a design checklist for digital environments: make beneficial action easy, expect user error, provide timely feedback, improve mappings, structure large option sets, keep opt-outs low-friction, and make incentives visible.
