# The Ethics of Influence - Chapter 6 Do People Like Nudges

## Core idea

Chapter 6 surveys empirical evidence about public support for nudges. Its main lesson is that people do not reject nudging as such. Support tends to depend on the perceived legitimacy of the goal, consistency with the chooser's values and interests, trust in the institution, and the type of psychological process targeted.

## Key claims

- Many people approve of nudges that promote widely shared goals and leave choice intact.
- People are less supportive when they distrust the actor, reject the goal, or suspect partisan, commercial, or self-serving motives.
- System 2 nudges that inform, remind, or prompt deliberation are generally easier to defend publicly than System 1 nudges that work through automatic processes.
- System 1 nudges are not categorically rejected, but they carry higher ethical and legitimacy burdens.
- Reactance matters. People may resist nudges that feel controlling, value-inconsistent, or manipulative.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital nudging often comes from private platforms, employers, insurers, apps, or opaque automated systems rather than public agencies. Chapter 6 therefore helps explain why legitimacy cannot be inferred from effectiveness. A design may change behavior and still lose public trust if users think it serves a hidden objective.

For the course, this chapter supports treating transparency and institutional trust as design constraints. It also helps distinguish a user-accepted nudge from a user-tolerated one: low complaint rates may reflect friction, ignorance, resignation, or lack of alternatives rather than genuine approval.
