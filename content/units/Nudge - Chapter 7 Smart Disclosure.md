# Nudge - Chapter 7 Smart Disclosure

## Place in the work

Chapter 7 is one of the final edition's most important additions for a Digital Nudging course. It moves from classic disclosure and labels to machine-readable data, user access to usage histories, and digital choice engines.

## Summary

The chapter argues that disclosure is a matter of [[Choice Architecture]], not merely a legal requirement to reveal information. Traditional disclosures often fail because they are long, technical, badly formatted, buried in fine print, or unavailable in forms that users and tools can process.

[[Smart Disclosure]] has two main components. First, complex information should be standardized and machine-readable so that software can compare options. Second, organizations that hold usage or purchase data about individuals should generally make those data available to the individuals themselves, so that third-party tools can help them make better decisions.

The chapter's vision is digital and infrastructural: when product attributes, prices, terms, fees, usage histories, and constraints are available in structured form, "choice engines" can search, compare, and recommend. Travel booking sites, open banking, energy-plan comparison apps, grocery-allergy filters, mortgage comparison tools, and smartphone-plan recommenders all illustrate the idea.

## Key claims

- Disclosure can be useless when it is formally available but practically unreadable.
- Standardized measurement is a precondition for meaningful comparison.
- Machine-readable disclosure lets software do work that ordinary consumers cannot reasonably do unaided.
- Users should often have access to their own usage data, because platforms learn from that history and can otherwise lock in consumers through informational advantage.
- Choice engines can improve welfare by translating complex markets into user-relevant recommendations.
- Choice engines themselves require governance because they may have incentives to rank, steer, or filter in self-serving ways.

## Evidence or method

The chapter uses examples from phone plans, fuel-economy reporting, APR standardization, fine print, online terms and conditions, travel booking, hotel fees, grocery allergens, Netflix usage history, UK Open Banking, and UK energy-market QR-code/choice-engine systems.

For the digital course, the central examples are terms and conditions as failed disclosure, user-owned usage data, Open Banking, and energy choice engines. These illustrate how disclosure becomes a digital infrastructure problem rather than a page-layout problem.

## Relation to other units

This chapter extends the "mapping" and "structure complex choices" principles from [[Nudge - Chapter 5 Choice Architecture]]. It also connects to [[Nudge - Chapter 8 Sludge]], because bad disclosure and hidden fees often function as friction, confusion, or lock-in.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Smart disclosure is a bridge from behavioral public policy to digital infrastructure. It asks how platforms, regulators, and third-party tools can make complex choices computable and comparable. For digital nudging, it shifts attention from isolated interface prompts to data access, interoperability, transparency, and choice engines that can act as user-serving choice architects.
