# Nudge - Part I Humans and Econs

## Place in the work

Part I provides the compact behavioral economics background for [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]]. It explains why real choosers are "nudge-able" without treating them as irrational or foolish. For the course, this unit should function as a behavioral economics primer rather than a comprehensive catalog of biases.

## Summary

Chapter 1, "Biases and Blunders," presents the central claim of [[Behavioral Economics]] in the book: people use sensible shortcuts that usually work, but in some environments produce predictable and systematic errors. The chapter covers anchoring, availability, representativeness, optimism and overconfidence, loss aversion, status quo bias, framing, and the Automatic/Reflective distinction.

Chapter 2, "Resisting Temptation," shifts from judgment to self-control. People can prefer one plan in a cool state and violate it in a hot state. The chapter uses the Planner/Doer model, commitment strategies, and mental accounting to explain why people sometimes welcome constraints, reminders, and account structures that look odd under the Econ model.

Chapter 3, "Following the Herd," explains social influence. People learn from others and seek social approval, but these forces can also produce conformity, cascades, pluralistic ignorance, identity-sensitive norm effects, and collective conservatism. Social norms can therefore be powerful nudges, especially when the relevant comparison group feels local, trusted, or identity-relevant.

## Key claims

- Behavioral economics does not require the claim that people are irrational. It starts from the milder and more useful claim that people are fallible and the world is hard.
- Heuristics economize on attention and calculation, but can produce bias when cues are misleading or environments are unfamiliar.
- Framing and status quo bias are directly relevant to interface design because the same substantive option can be experienced differently depending on wording, ordering, default status, and effort.
- Self-control problems make some friction welfare-enhancing when chosen by the user, as in commitment devices, but harmful when imposed to trap the user.
- Social influence works through information, peer pressure, identity, and norm perception. It can support public goods or amplify misinformation, herd behavior, and manipulation.
- Digital environments increase the scale and speed of these mechanisms: visible metrics, recommendation counts, defaults, autoplay, preselected options, renewal systems, and social proof can all steer Humans.

## Evidence or method

The unit uses classic behavioral demonstrations and vivid examples. The most reusable examples for a Digital Nudging course are the taxi tip screen for anchoring, streaming autoplay and automatic renewals for status quo bias, the cashew bowl and commitment devices for self-control, and Asch-style conformity or social norm messages for social influence.

Other examples are useful but secondary: visual illusions, the Linda problem, optimism about grades or divorce, Clocky, Stickk, Christmas clubs, mental accounts, "Don't Mess with Texas," tax-compliance letters, hotel towel reuse, and social cascades around same-sex marriage. They should be treated as teaching illustrations rather than the center of the digital course.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital nudging depends on the behavioral regularities summarized here. Online users are busy, distracted, socially embedded, and often choosing under uncertainty. Digital interfaces can anchor expectations, exploit inertia, trigger social comparison, simplify good choices, or make mistakes more likely. This unit is therefore the course's behavioral foundation, but it should be taught as a mechanism map: which features of digital environments activate which human tendencies?
