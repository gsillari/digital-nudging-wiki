# Nudge - Chapter 4 When Do We Need a Nudge

## Place in the work

Chapter 4 turns the behavioral account from [[Nudge - Part I Humans and Econs]] into a practical diagnostic test. It asks when choice architects should intervene, how strongly, and why markets alone may not protect choosers.

## Summary

The chapter's golden rule is to offer nudges that are likely to help and unlikely to harm. Nudges are especially appropriate when choices require scarce attention, are difficult, are unfamiliar or rare, lack prompt feedback, involve delayed benefits or costs, or require people to translate technical features into lived experience.

The chapter emphasizes memory and attention first. Reminders, checklists, and implementation-intention prompts can help because people often intend to act but fail to attend at the right moment. Technology makes such cues easier to deliver, especially through phones, texts, calendars, and appointment systems.

It then identifies several choice conditions that call for help: investment goods with immediate costs and delayed benefits, temptation goods with immediate benefits and delayed costs, complex decisions, infrequent decisions, poor feedback environments, and choices whose consequences are hard to imagine. The final section argues that markets are mixed: competition can help, but firms may also profit by catering to biases, scams, inattention, addiction, and overoptimism.

## Key claims

- A nudge is most justified when choosers are likely to make mistakes and the intervention can help with little downside.
- Scarce attention makes reminders, prompts, and checklists central tools of choice architecture.
- Delayed consequences make self-control problems predictable: people may underinvest in long-term goods and overconsume temptation goods.
- Difficulty and rarity matter because people cannot learn well from decisions they make only once or twice.
- Feedback is often asymmetric: people see the results of chosen options but rarely learn what would have happened under rejected options.
- Mapping problems arise when people cannot connect an option's attributes to their future welfare, as with health plans, mortgages, or technical product features.
- Markets can amplify rather than solve behavioral problems when there is money to be made from confusion, unrealistic optimism, addiction, or inertia.

## Evidence or method

The chapter uses implementation-intention voter prompts, surgical and regulatory checklists, investment and temptation goods, rare high-stakes decisions, poor feedback examples, and snake-oil markets. For the digital course, the strongest examples are phone-based reminders, online appointment prompts, hard-to-compare financial and health products, and internet markets for dubious goods.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This chapter can be used as the course's diagnostic checklist for digital interventions. A digital nudge is more defensible when users face scarce attention, complex options, delayed consequences, poor feedback, or unclear mappings. It is less defensible when the platform is merely exploiting the same vulnerabilities for retention, revenue, data extraction, or lock-in. The chapter therefore helps separate welfare-oriented design from manipulative digital steering.
