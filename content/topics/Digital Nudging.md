# Digital Nudging

## Scope

[[Digital Nudging]] is the course hub for sources and concepts about behavioral intervention in digital environments. It organizes work on online choice architecture, interface design, personalization, recommender systems, dark patterns, sludge, autonomy, welfare, privacy, experimentation, platform governance, and AI-mediated persuasion.

## Foundational Sources

- [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]]: foundational book for the course vocabulary, especially [[Nudge]], [[Choice Architecture]], [[Behavioral Economics]], [[Defaults]], [[Smart Disclosure]], and [[Sludge]].
- [[Behavioural Insights Team, 2024|EAST: Four Simple Ways to Apply Behavioural Insights]]: compact practitioner reference for designing behavioral interventions that are easy, attractive, social, and timely.
- [[Sunstein, 2016|The Ethics of Influence: Government in the Age of Behavioral Science]]: ethics framework for evaluating nudges through welfare, autonomy, dignity, self-government, transparency, and manipulation.
- [[Hansen and Jespersen, 2013|Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice]]: definitional and ethical framework that classifies nudges by cognitive target and epistemic transparency.
- [[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge: Big Data as a Mode of Regulation by Design]]: cornerstone digital source for hypernudging, personalization, continuous modification, recommendation systems, privacy, opacity, and platform governance.
- [[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]]: programmatic source for the positive potential of algorithmic nudging as thought partnership, reflective-equilibrium support, adaptive armor, and debiasing.
- [[Mills, 2022|Personalized Nudging (Mills)]]: systematic complement that distinguishes delivery personalization from content or target personalization.
- [[Gray et al., 2018|The Dark (Patterns) Side of UX Design]]: early HCI taxonomy showing how human-centered design values can be co-opted for deceptive or exploitative interface design.
- [[Mathur et al., 2019|Dark Patterns at Scale]]: empirical anchor for dark patterns in e-commerce, including prevalence, categories, and third-party dark-pattern services.
- [[Narayanan et al., 2020|Dark Patterns: Past, Present, and Future]]: compact genealogy connecting retail deception, behavioral nudging, growth hacking, A/B testing, and extraction of money, data, and attention.
- [[Mathur et al., 2021|What Makes a Dark Pattern... Dark?]]: normative and measurement framework for dark-pattern attributes, welfare, autonomy, collective harms, and regulatory objectives.
- [[Luguri and Strahilevitz, 2021|Shining a Light on Dark Patterns]]: experimental and legal source showing that mild dark patterns can strongly shift choices while avoiding backlash.
- [[Gray et al., 2024|An Ontology of Dark Patterns Knowledge]]: harmonized ontology that organizes dark patterns into high-, meso-, and low-level patterns for research and regulation.
- [[Angwin et al., 2016|Machine Bias]]: opening case for algorithmic fairness, using COMPAS to show how risk scores can steer institutional decisions while distributing error burdens unequally.
- [[Kleinberg et al., 2016|Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores]]: first formal fairness result, showing why calibration and error-balance criteria cannot generally be satisfied together.
- [[Hedden, 2021|On Statistical Criteria of Algorithmic Fairness]]: philosophical defense of calibration and predictive parity as fundamental criteria for fair prediction.
- [[Hellman, 2020|Measuring Algorithmic Fairness]]: legal argument that fairness is usually about action and error burdens, making error-rate and error-ratio criteria central.
- [[Binns, 2018|Fairness in Machine Learning: Lessons from Political Philosophy]]: bridge from fairness metrics to normative theories of discrimination, equality, justice, and representation.
- [[Corbett-Davies et al., 2017|Algorithmic Decision Making and the Cost of Fairness]]: policy-alignment source showing that fairness constraints should be evaluated against regulatory goals and decision costs.
- [[Narayanan, 2026|What If Algorithmic Fairness Is a Category Error?]]: sociotechnical corrective arguing that fairness belongs to whole algorithmic bureaucracies, not to predictive models alone.
- [[Kleinberg et al., 2018|Human Decisions and Machine Predictions]]: core efficiency-branch source showing that algorithmic bail decision support can reduce crime at fixed jail rates or reduce jail at fixed crime rates.
- [[Ludwig and Mullainathan, 2021|Fragile Algorithms and Fallible Decision-Makers]]: implementation and governance source explaining why algorithmic gains require handling selective labels, omitted payoffs, and human overrides.
- [[Mullainathan, 2025|Economics in the Age of Algorithms]]: summary source for prediction policy problems, algorithms as decision aids, and algorithms as empirical and intervention tools.
- [[Ludwig et al., 2024|The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Algorithms]]: effectiveness source arguing that algorithmic interventions can be highly cost-effective when they improve ranking and scale cheaply.
- [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2017|Regulating by Robot]]: legal-compatibility source for algorithmic adjudication, rulemaking, due process, nondelegation, and transparency.
- [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2019|Transparency and Algorithmic Governance]]: transparency source distinguishing fishbowl transparency from reasoned transparency.
- [[Coglianese, 2021|Administrative Law in the Automated State]]: automated-state source arguing that AI can advance expert administration and accountability, but must preserve empathy.
- [[Coglianese and Crum, 2024|Taking Training Seriously]]: management-based-regulation source arguing for human involvement during AI training and development.
- [[Coglianese and Crum, 2025b|Regulating Multifunctionality]]: foundation-model governance source explaining why multifunctional AI defeats one-size-fits-all regulation.
- [[Coglianese and Crum, 2025a|Leashes, Not Guardrails]]: governance metaphor and risk-regulation source for flexible, human-held AI oversight.
- [[Nudging and the Ethics of Nudging]]: course synthesis connecting the definition of nudging to chooser welfare, autonomy, dignity, transparency, manipulation, defaults, sludge, and digital accountability.
- [[Nudge - Introduction]]: starting point for what nudges are and why no choice environment is neutral.
- [[Nudge - Part I Humans and Econs]]: compact behavioral economics background.
- [[Nudge - Chapter 4 When Do We Need a Nudge]]: diagnostic checklist for when nudges are useful.
- [[Nudge - Chapter 5 Choice Architecture]]: design toolkit for choice architects.
- [[Nudge - Chapter 7 Smart Disclosure]] and [[Nudge - Chapter 8 Sludge]]: most directly digital-relevant chapters in the first source.
- [[The Ethics of Influence - Chapter 3 As Judged by Themselves]], [[The Ethics of Influence - Chapter 4 Values]], and [[The Ethics of Influence - Chapter 5 Fifty Shades of Manipulation]]: ethical core for chooser welfare, values, and manipulation.

## Current synthesis

The first foundation from [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]] is that digital environments are always choice architectures. A platform cannot avoid arranging options, setting defaults, making some actions easier than others, and deciding what users notice. The central question is therefore not whether digital systems influence users, but whether that influence is user-serving, transparent, contestable, and easy to resist.

[[Nudging and the Ethics of Nudging]] now collects the opening course argument: nudging begins from unavoidable choice architecture and human fallibility, but it becomes legitimate only under ethical constraints such as chooser welfare, autonomy, dignity, transparency, anti-manipulation, practical exit, and accountability.

The second foundation is behavioral: users are Humans, not Econs. They have limited attention, rely on heuristics, follow defaults, respond to frames, struggle with self-control, and take cues from others. Digital systems intensify these mechanisms because they can personalize, automate, test, and scale choice architecture.

The third foundation is ethical and institutional. Helpful nudges should preserve meaningful exit and improve choices by the chooser's own lights. When design hides costs, makes exit hard, buries privacy controls, or forces users through burdensome flows, the framework shifts from nudging to [[Sludge]] or dark patterns.

[[EAST Framework]] adds a practical intervention lens: digital nudges should reduce unnecessary effort, make relevant information salient, use social context carefully, and arrive when users can act. It also adds a methodological caution: context, replication, unintended effects, and evaluation matter.

[[Sunstein, 2016|The Ethics of Influence: Government in the Age of Behavioral Science]] now supplies the course's normative spine. A digital nudge should be assessed through welfare, autonomy, dignity, and self-government, with special concern for [[Manipulation]] and [[Transparency and Publicity]]. This matters more in digital contexts because proprietary interfaces can hide the relevant architecture, data use, optimization target, and experimental evidence.

[[Hansen and Jespersen, 2013|Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice]] adds a diagnostic matrix for that ethics work. It separates transparent from non-transparent nudges and automatic-behavior nudges from reflective-choice nudges. This is especially useful for digital systems because a platform can disclose a general policy while hiding the operative mechanism in ranking, personalization, default selection, or interface experiments.

The dark-pattern cluster gives the negative mirror of this framework. [[Gray et al., 2018|Gray et al.]], [[Mathur et al., 2019|Mathur et al.]], [[Narayanan et al., 2020|Narayanan et al.]], [[Mathur et al., 2021|Mathur et al.]], [[Luguri and Strahilevitz, 2021|Luguri and Strahilevitz]], and [[Gray et al., 2024|Gray et al.]] show how digital choice architecture becomes [[Adversarial Choice Architecture]]. Dark patterns are not simply crude or annoying nudges. They are interface strategies that use friction, urgency, social proof, hidden information, bad defaults, forced action, and A/B-tested presentation to extract money, data, or attention. [[Sludge]] is one frequent method, especially in cancellation and privacy mazes, but the deeper issue is the reversal of purpose: the choice environment is built for the service's objectives against the user's welfare, autonomy, privacy, or reflective preference.

[[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge: Big Data as a Mode of Regulation by Design]] marks the course's digital pivot. Digital nudging is not just classic nudging delivered through an app or website. In Yeung's account, [[Hypernudge|hypernudges]] are Big Data-driven systems that personalize the informational choice environment, continuously update it through feedback loops, and operate through recommendation, ranking, and guidance systems. This creates the course's central digital themes: personalization, A/B testing and optimization, opaque manipulation, recommender systems, algorithmic accountability and fairness, privacy, and dark patterns.

[[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]] adds the constructive counterpart. If hypernudging shows how algorithmic choice architecture can become opaque control, Behavioral Economics 2.0 asks how algorithms could become tools users work with: explaining options, filling informational blind spots, highlighting biases, implementing chosen plans, protecting users in adversarial markets, and training better judgment. This expands the course beyond nudges as interventions toward nudges, agents, and decision aids as possible instruments of agency.

[[Mills, 2022|Personalized Nudging (Mills)]] gives this transition a useful middle layer. It distinguishes personalizing the delivery of a nudge from personalizing the content or target of the nudge. That makes it easier to compare [[Precision Nudging]], [[Hypernudge]], and [[Behavioral Economics 2.0]]: they may all personalize, but they personalize different parts of the intervention problem.

The algorithmic fairness module now begins with [[Angwin et al., 2016|Machine Bias]], [[Kleinberg et al., 2016|Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores]], [[Hedden, 2021|On Statistical Criteria of Algorithmic Fairness]], [[Hellman, 2020|Measuring Algorithmic Fairness]], [[Binns, 2018|Fairness in Machine Learning: Lessons from Political Philosophy]], [[Corbett-Davies et al., 2017|Algorithmic Decision Making and the Cost of Fairness]], [[Narayanan, 2026|What If Algorithmic Fairness Is a Category Error?]], [[Kleinberg et al., 2018|Human Decisions and Machine Predictions]], [[Ludwig and Mullainathan, 2021|Fragile Algorithms and Fallible Decision-Makers]], [[Mullainathan, 2025|Economics in the Age of Algorithms]], and [[Ludwig et al., 2024|The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Algorithms]]. The COMPAS controversy grounds the issue in a concrete institutional workflow: risk scores shape human decisions even when they do not formally decide. Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan then show why fairness cannot be reduced to a single technical repair. When base rates differ and prediction is imperfect, calibration and equalized error burdens generally conflict. Hedden adds that calibration or [[Predictive Parity]] is nonetheless fundamental for fair prediction because scores should have the same evidential meaning across groups. Hellman answers from law: fairness usually concerns action, so [[Error Rate Parity]] and the cost of mistakes matter more directly than epistemic parity. Binns then supplies the broader normative frame: the choice of fairness metric should be justified by political philosophy and by the domain's specific goods, harms, histories, and forms of representation. Corbett-Davies et al. add the policy layer: fairness constraints should be assessed against the regulatory goal and the decision costs they impose. Narayanan then adds the sociotechnical correction: fairness is not finally a property of the score, classifier, or recommender, but of the whole decision system in which it operates. The efficiency branch then asks whether algorithmic decision support can improve that system's frontier: less crime with the same incarceration, or less incarceration with the same crime, while accounting for selective labels, omitted payoffs, and human overrides. Mullainathan's AEA lecture generalizes this branch as the study of [[Prediction Policy Problems]], while Ludwig, Mullainathan, and Rambachan reinforce the effectiveness argument through MVPF and scale.

This creates two course branches. The bias branch asks how to define unfairness once parity metrics conflict, moving from mathematical definitions to consequences, political philosophy, regulation, and sociotechnical systems. The efficiency branch asks whether algorithmic prediction can still improve institutional decisions, especially by moving the crime/incarceration frontier and by improving prediction-dependent rankings in other domains. The digital-nudging payoff is that ranking, targeting, personalization, and recommendation must be audited as whole choice architectures, not merely as models with fairness scores or prediction accuracy numbers.

The algorithmic-governance cluster adds a third institutional layer. [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2017|Regulating by Robot]] and [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2019|Transparency and Algorithmic Governance]] show why algorithmic public administration is not automatically unlawful or illegitimate: agencies can specify objectives, validate tools, preserve due process, and provide [[Reasoned Transparency]] even when models are technically complex. [[Coglianese, 2021|Administrative Law in the Automated State]] then adds the legitimacy condition that automation must remain empathic. The later management papers, [[Coglianese and Crum, 2024|Taking Training Seriously]], [[Coglianese and Crum, 2025b|Regulating Multifunctionality]], and [[Coglianese and Crum, 2025a|Leashes, Not Guardrails]], explain why modern AI needs flexible lifecycle governance. Because foundation models are heterogeneous, multifunctional, and continuously changing, the right regulatory question is often whether an organization has a serious risk-management system, human-guided training, auditability, and humans with a real grip on the leash.

For digital nudging, this means that ethicality is not only a property of a single interface intervention. It is also a property of the system that trains, targets, tests, explains, monitors, and revises the intervention. AI assistants, recommender systems, adaptive defaults, and public-service chatbots should be evaluated through their objectives, validation, human oversight, reasoned transparency, contestability, and capacity for empathy.

## Open Questions

- How should the course measure user welfare when engagement, revealed preference, satisfaction, and long-term well-being point in different directions?
- Which digital nudges count as transparent enough when their effects depend on salience, defaults, or inattention?
- How should governance distinguish helpful friction from [[Sludge]]?
- How do personalization and AI-mediated interfaces change the classic nudge criteria of easy avoidance, transparency, and user-centered welfare?
- How should proprietary platforms be held accountable for nudges whose objectives, data, and behavioral mechanisms are not visible to users?
- How should continuous experimentation be governed when digital platforms can modify choice architecture in real time?
- When does a welfare-promoting digital nudge become an extractive dark pattern, especially when the same mechanism can be tested, personalized, and made subtle?
- How should regulators treat sludge when it preserves formal choice but defeats practical refusal, cancellation, or privacy protection?
- Can algorithmic thought partners be designed to preserve agency while still correcting mistakes, and how would we know they move users toward [[Reflective Equilibrium]]?
- When digital systems personalize both delivery and target, what governance is needed to keep the target user-serving?
- Which fairness metric should govern a digital intervention when calibration, error parity, and policy goals point in different directions?
- When does algorithmic decision support count as a nudge, and when does its institutional power make that framing too soft?
- What kind of management-based oversight is needed when a foundation model becomes a personalized choice architect?
- How can automated digital nudging remain empathic when users need to be heard rather than merely optimized?
