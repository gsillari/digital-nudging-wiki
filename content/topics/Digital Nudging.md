# Digital Nudging

## Scope

[[Digital Nudging]] is the course hub for behavioral intervention in digital environments. It connects classic choice architecture to interface design, personalization, recommender systems, dark patterns, algorithmic fairness, privacy, experimentation, platform governance, and AI-mediated decision support.

## Source clusters

The foundation is [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]], sharpened by [[EAST Framework]], [[Sunstein, 2016|The Ethics of Influence]], [[Hansen and Jespersen, 2013|Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice]], and [[Loewenstein and Chater, 2017|Putting Nudges in Perspective]]. These sources give the opening vocabulary: [[Nudge]], [[Choice Architecture]], [[Behavioral Economics]], [[Chooser Welfare]], [[Manipulation]], [[Transparency and Disclosure]], and [[Sludge]].

Loewenstein and Chater add the policy-toolkit frame for the opening module. Digital choice architecture can help, but digital nudging should not be treated as a substitute for conventional policy when harms arise from platform incentives, market structure, data extraction, privacy externalities, or deceptive design.

The digital pivot begins with [[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge]], then branches in two directions. One direction is critical: [[Dark Patterns as Adversarial Choice Architecture]] tracks how online choice architecture becomes extraction-oriented interface design. The other is constructive: [[Ludwig et al., 2025|Behavioral Economics 2.0]], [[Personalized Choice Architecture]], and [[AI as Choice Aid]] ask how algorithmic systems might support agency, reflection, and better decision-making rather than merely exploiting behavioral regularities.

The choice-aid branch begins from the [[Behavior-Welfare Gap]]: observed behavior is not always reliable evidence of welfare. [[Kling et al., 2012|Comparison friction]], [[Bhargava et al., 2017|dominated health-plan choices]], [[Shrouded Attributes]], [[Kleinberg et al., 2023|engagement optimization]], and [[Chang et al., 2024|quantification fixation]] show different ways choice can diverge from welfare. AI becomes course-relevant when it can turn disclosure and data into trusted, personalized sense-making.

The algorithmic branch uses [[Predictive Algorithms, Bias, and Fairness]] to connect digital nudging to prediction, scoring, ranking, and decision support. The COMPAS controversy and [[Fairness Impossibility Results]] make clear that there is no neutral metric-only settlement; the course therefore moves through predictive parity, error burdens, political philosophy, policy alignment, sociotechnical systems, and efficiency-frontier arguments.

The governance layer is collected in [[Algorithmic Governance, Management, and the Automated State]] and anchored by [[Algorithmic Governance]]. It matters because digital nudges are increasingly produced by AI systems whose objectives, training, testing, personalization, and monitoring are institutional questions, not just interface-design questions.

## Working questions

The course keeps returning to a small set of questions: whose welfare is optimized, how visible the steering is, whether refusal is practically easy, what data and experiments drive the intervention, how fairness should be defined when metrics conflict, and what oversight is needed when a digital system becomes a personalized choice architect.
