# Luguri and Strahilevitz, 2021: Shining a Light on Dark Patterns

## Summary

[[Luguri and Strahilevitz, 2021|Shining a Light on Dark Patterns]] provides the strongest legal and experimental evidence in this ingest cluster. The article studies how dark patterns affect consumer decisions and uses those findings to assess consumer-protection law.

The paper is crucial because it shows that dark patterns can be effective even when they are mild enough to avoid backlash. That matters for digital nudging because the most dangerous manipulations may be the ones that remain beneath the user's threshold of complaint.

## Key findings

- In the first experiment, the control condition had an 11.3 percent acceptance rate, mild dark patterns raised acceptance to 25.8 percent, and aggressive dark patterns raised it to 41.9 percent.
- Mild dark patterns more than doubled signups while largely avoiding consumer backlash.
- Aggressive dark patterns produced stronger effects but also more backlash.
- Less educated subjects were more susceptible to mild dark patterns.
- Price and stakes did not significantly change acceptance in the studied setup; decision architecture drove behavior more than price.
- In the second experiment, hidden information, obstruction, and trick questions were especially effective.

## Legal argument

The paper treats dark patterns as a consumer-protection problem, not only a design problem. It argues that many dark patterns may be unfair, deceptive, or otherwise legally problematic. It also proposes that regulators can use firms' own A/B tests and internal experiments as evidence of manipulation or consumer harm.

This is important for the vault's continuous optimization and experimentation ([[Hypernudge]]) page: optimization data can be a source of accountability as well as a source of manipulation.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This paper is the best evidence for the course's point that decision architecture can dominate ordinary market signals. If a mild interface manipulation doubles acceptance while price changes do not move decisions much, then the "choice" being observed is partly a product of the architecture.

It also clarifies why sludge is a method rather than the definition of a dark pattern. Obstruction and hidden information are effective techniques, but the deeper issue is that the design exploits cognitive limits to produce a result users would not endorse under clearer conditions.
