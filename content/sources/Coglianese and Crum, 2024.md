# Coglianese and Crum, 2024: Taking Training Seriously

## Summary

[[Coglianese and Crum, 2024|Taking Training Seriously]] connects emerging AI regulation to the technical stage at which models are trained and developed. Coglianese and Crum argue that major governance frameworks, including the EU AI Act, U.S. executive initiatives, and ISO risk-management standards, share a [[Management-Based AI Regulation]] logic: instead of trying to specify every permitted or forbidden AI output in advance, they require organizations to build internal systems for identifying, managing, documenting, and mitigating risks.

The paper's distinctive move is to say that this regulatory model should push oversight upstream. Human oversight should not be treated only as post-deployment monitoring, user review, or after-the-fact audit. For high-stakes AI, the training process itself should be designed to include human guidance.

## Key claims

- AI is heterogeneous and dynamic enough that rigid, prescriptive regulation will often miss the relevant risks.
- Management-based regulation is becoming the practical governance paradigm because it requires developers and deployers to maintain internal risk-management systems.
- Human oversight is most effective when it shapes training and development, not only deployment.
- Human-guided training can improve model performance by adding human intuition, domain judgment, salience, and evaluation criteria that data-only training may miss.
- Human-guided training can also support fairness and explainability because it can align models with human-recognizable reasons, examples, and errors.
- High-stakes contexts should lean more heavily on human-guided training than low-stakes contexts because the costs of misalignment, opacity, and unfairness are larger.

## Course use

Use this paper as the bridge between governance and model construction. It helps the course avoid a narrow view of "oversight" as a dashboard, audit, or appeal button added after a system is already built. For digital nudging, the equivalent point is that ethical governance should enter the design, training, ranking, personalization, and testing pipeline itself.

This paper also gives a constructive complement to the darker [[Hypernudge]] concerns. If digital choice architecture becomes adaptive and algorithmic, the response is not only disclosure or prohibition. One response is to require human judgment and domain-specific values to be built into the development process.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital nudging systems are increasingly trained systems: recommender models, ranking models, personalization engines, generative assistants, and decision-support tools. If their behavioral effects are shaped during training, then governance that begins only after deployment arrives too late.

The paper supplies a strong principle for the course: digital nudges should be governed across the lifecycle, including data selection, training feedback, objective choice, evaluation, deployment, monitoring, and revision.
