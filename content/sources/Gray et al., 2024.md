# Gray et al., 2024: An Ontology of Dark Patterns Knowledge

## Summary

[[Gray et al., 2024|An Ontology of Dark Patterns Knowledge]] is the most systematic taxonomy source in this cluster. The paper harmonizes academic and regulatory dark-pattern taxonomies into an ontology that can support shared research, regulation, detection, and enforcement.

The paper matters because the dark-pattern literature has many overlapping lists. A shared ontology makes it possible to distinguish general strategies from middle-level mechanisms and low-level interface executions.

## Key contribution

The ontology distinguishes three levels:

- High-level patterns: general strategies that limit, impair, or undermine autonomy, decision-making, or free choice.
- Meso-level patterns: content-agnostic "angles of attack" that explain how the strategy works.
- Low-level patterns: situated interface executions that can often be identified manually or algorithmically.

The paper groups the ontology under five high-level strategies: obstruction, sneaking, interface interference, forced action, and social engineering.

## Examples of the hierarchy

- Obstruction includes roach motel, creating barriers, adding steps, and privacy mazes.
- Sneaking includes bait and switch, disguised ads, hiding information, hidden costs, partitioned pricing, and reference pricing.
- Interface interference includes false hierarchy, bad defaults, visual prominence, bundling, pressured selling, trick questions, choice overload, and ambiguous feedforward.
- Forced action includes nagging, forced continuity, forced registration, forced communication or disclosure, Privacy Zuckering, friend spam, address book leeching, social pyramid schemes, and gamification.
- Social engineering includes scarcity and popularity claims, social proof, low-stock and high-demand messages, testimonials, urgency, countdown timers, limited-time messages, shaming, confirmshaming, and personalization.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This source gives the course a vocabulary for moving from examples to structure. Examples such as a countdown timer, hidden fee, preselected box, or cancellation maze are useful, but the ontology lets us ask what family of steering is operating and why it matters.

It also helps link dark patterns to digital governance. Regulators and researchers need stable categories if they are going to audit interfaces, compare findings, detect patterns automatically, and decide which designs violate consumer-protection or data-protection goals.
