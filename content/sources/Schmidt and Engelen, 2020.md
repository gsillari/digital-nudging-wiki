# Schmidt and Engelen, 2020: The Ethics of Nudging: An Overview

## Role in the vault

[[Schmidt and Engelen, 2020|The Ethics of Nudging: An Overview]] is an excellent review map for the ethical debate around nudging. Its main value is not a new ethical test, but an organized account of what the major objections are, how defenders of nudging respond, and why no single objection defeats the nudge program as a whole.

The review is especially useful for the course because it turns the broad worry that nudges undermine autonomy into a set of more precise questions. A nudge can preserve external freedom of choice while still raising concerns about whether the chooser's action reflects their own ends, whether the intervention respects rational agency, and whether the choice architect exercises uncontrolled power.

## Core argument

The case for nudging rests on several familiar claims: nudges can be cost-effective, evidence-driven, comparatively freedom-preserving, politically attractive, and responsive to the inevitability of [[Choice Architecture]]. But the inevitability argument is not a blank check. Deliberate choice architecture is ethically different from accidental influence because intentional design brings responsibility for goals, mechanisms, evidence, avoidability, side effects, and institutional control.

The review's conclusion is deliberately balanced. There is no knockdown objection showing that nudging as a whole should be rejected. At the same time, the objections are not distractions. They identify the criteria that should guide case-by-case assessment of particular nudges.

## Ethical objection map

The review divides autonomy objections into four dimensions:

- Freedom of choice: do options remain externally available, and is avoidance practically possible?
- Volitional autonomy: does the nudge help people act on their own ends, or does it make their choices less genuinely theirs?
- Rational agency: does the nudge bypass or exploit deliberation, or can it improve the match between people, decision procedures, and environments?
- Non-domination: is the architect's power transparent, democratically controlled, and contestable, or does it become uncontrolled influence over citizens or users?

This map clarifies why "options remain open" is not enough. A nudge can preserve formal choice while still raising concerns about ownership of ends, rational respect, opacity, or domination.

The review then treats [[Manipulation]] and dignity as worries that are best decomposed through the earlier autonomy questions. If manipulation is troubling because it works behind people's backs, exploits vulnerability, bypasses rational capacities, or infantilizes adults, the response must address transparency, rational agency, autonomy, and non-domination rather than simply insisting that choice remains formally free.

## Ends and structural reform

Some objections concern the goals nudges pursue rather than the psychological route by which they work. The epistemic worry is whether policymakers know what is good for people. The moral worry is whether governments should promote contested ends in pluralist societies. The review's response is comparative: these questions apply to many policy tools, not only nudges, and nudges may sometimes be preferable because they preserve opt-out. But easy opt-out can also make paternalistic expansion seem too easy, so transparency and democratic control remain important.

The structural-reform objection is equally important. Nudging can be misused as a light-touch substitute for stronger action on socioeconomic, institutional, or market causes of harm. The best response is not to deny the problem, but to treat nudging as one policy instrument among others. Nudges can be combined with structural reform, used in nonideal contexts where stronger reform is unavailable, or directed at structural mismatches between human decision-making and intentionally designed environments.

## Digital choice architecture

The review explicitly identifies digital choice architecture as a fertile and underdeveloped area for future ethics work. Digital nudges are especially powerful because firms can collect user data, run A/B tests, use algorithms, personalize interventions, and shape behavior at scale. They are also especially difficult to scrutinize because technical details, optimization objectives, and machine-learning decisions may be opaque to users and outsiders.

This makes the source a bridge from classical nudge ethics to [[Hypernudge]], [[Dark Patterns]], [[Privacy and Consent]], and platform governance. The public-private contrast becomes central: public nudges may be transparent and democratically controlled, while private digital choice architecture may be proprietary, personalized, and optimized for attention, conversion, or data extraction.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

The review gives the course a compact ethical diagnostic for digital nudges:

- Do not ask only whether options remain available; ask whether choice remains meaningful across autonomy, rational agency, and domination.
- Do not treat transparency as a generic notice; ask whether users and publics can understand, contest, and control the relevant influence.
- Do not treat manipulation as a vague insult; ask what kind of bypassing, opacity, vulnerability exploitation, or dignity failure is alleged.
- Do not let the light-touch appeal of nudging displace stronger remedies when the problem is structural, exploitative, or market-made.
- Do not evaluate digital choice architecture only at the visible interface; include data collection, experimentation, personalization, algorithmic opacity, and institutional incentives.

This source therefore strengthens [[Ethics of Nudging]] as a review framework and helps prepare the move from classical nudging to digital nudging.
