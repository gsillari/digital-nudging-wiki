# Chang et al., 2024: Does Counting Change What Counts? Quantification Fixation Biases Decision-Making

## Summary

[[Chang et al., 2024|Does Counting Change What Counts?]] studies how numeric representation changes decisions involving tradeoffs. Across 21 experiments, people tend to favor the option that dominates on the dimension presented numerically, even when the competing qualitative dimension is equally relevant.

The authors call this pattern quantification fixation ([[Behavior-Welfare Gap]]). Its mechanism is comparison fluency: numbers are easier to compare than qualitative descriptions, pictures, or visualizations, so decision-makers overweight the quantified attribute.

## Key claims

- Quantification changes what counts in decision-making.
- When one dimension of a tradeoff is numeric and another is qualitative, people privilege the numeric dimension.
- The effect appears across managerial, policy, consumer, hiring, and charitable donation contexts.
- Quantification fixation persists even when qualitative information is familiar or explicitly mapped to numeric values.
- The effect has real consequences in incentive-compatible hiring and donation tasks.
- Comparison fluency is a key mechanism: numeric information feels easier to compare.
- People with higher subjective numeracy are more susceptible because they feel more comfortable using numbers.

## Evidence and methods

The paper reports 21 preregistered experiments with more than 23,000 participants. Main-text studies include hotel, restaurant, hiring, donation, and policy scenarios. The authors randomize which tradeoff dimension is represented numerically and which is represented qualitatively.

The effect is not explained by recall, precision, familiarity, or ambiguity alone. Making numeric information less fluent attenuates the effect, and subjective numeracy moderates it.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital choice environments constantly translate the world into numbers: ratings, stars, scores, ranks, percentages, counts, dashboards, risk estimates, prices, and algorithmic confidence. This paper warns that quantification is not neutral information architecture. It changes salience and weight.

For [[AI as Choice Aid]], the lesson is subtle. A choice aid should simplify and compare, but it should not make only the numeric dimensions fluent. If an AI system makes price, risk, efficiency, popularity, or predicted satisfaction vivid while leaving dignity, fit, quality, care, or meaning qualitative and vague, it may systematically skew choice.
