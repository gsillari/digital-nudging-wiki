# Sunstein, 2026: Paternalism after Behavioral Economics

## Summary

Behavioral evidence of error challenges the assumption that leaving people alone always serves their welfare, but it does not establish a universal case for intervention. The relevant comparison is between fallible choosers and fallible architects, under particular conditions of knowledge, heterogeneity, learning, and agency. Sunstein brings coercive paternalism, [[Libertarian Paternalism]], and antipaternalism into that comparison, defending nudges as often preferable without treating them as the right instrument in every setting.

The paper's distinctive final move is to distinguish choosing an option from choosing how a decision will be made. People can exercise agency by delegating to a default, an expert, or AI. Requiring active choice can itself be paternalistic when it overrides a person's preference to delegate.

## Error, knowledge, and the three approaches

Mill's epistemic defense of liberty rests on the chooser's superior knowledge of their own circumstances. Behavioral findings put that defense under pressure: someone may choose a dominated option or fail to select suitable means to an accepted end. But the intervention must also pass an epistemic test. Officials and designers can misunderstand preferences, suffer biases, or respond to interests other than the chooser's (pp. 1-2, 4-6).

Coercive paternalists argue that serious, predictable errors can warrant mandates and bans. Libertarian paternalists favor assistance that preserves the ability to reject the recommendation. Antipaternalists question both the diagnosis of error and the architect's knowledge or legitimacy; some favor strengthening decision competences instead. These are families of presumptions rather than mutually exclusive policy packages (sections 2-4).

Both paternalist camps generally defend means paternalism: helping people achieve their own ends. The distinction from imposing ends is unstable, however. Discouraging smoking looks different depending on whether the person's end is described as smoking or living well. Describing an end very broadly can conceal disagreement about what the person actually values (p. 2).

## Choosing an instrument

The welfare framework requires several conditions together, not simply evidence of a bias (section 5.1, pp. 5-6):

- Coercion has a stronger case when all or too many choosers make serious mistakes, architects know the better option, and people cannot readily be helped to choose well.
- Nudging has a stronger case when many people err, architects know an effective corrective, and heterogeneity means a mandate or ban would harm others. Opt-out protects diversity only when those who reject the nudge can do so for sensible reasons.
- Nonintervention, potentially with competence-building, has a stronger case when people generally choose well and planners lack knowledge or are biased. Feasible, valuable learning can also count against either form of paternalism.

Welfare does not exhaust the argument. Rights, respect, and autonomy can constrain interventions even when aggregate benefits exceed costs. Evaluating those objections requires attention to how an intervention works (section 5.2).

## Learning, nudges, and boosts

Educative nudges supply information or prompts, such as labels and reminders. Architectural nudges alter the decision environment without necessarily teaching anything, as with automatic enrollment. Boosts aim to improve underlying skills, knowledge, or cognitive tools, such as statistical literacy. Their proponents distinguish these more general competences from a label that helps with one decision; the boundary remains debated (pp. 3-5).

Learning is valuable, but it costs time and attention. Sunstein asks which concrete problems competence-building can realistically solve and whether requiring everyone to master every decision domain is itself burdensome. This makes the choice between assistance and learning an empirical and ethical question rather than an automatic preference for either.

## Agency includes delegation

First-order agency concerns making a choice; second-order agency concerns how one wants choices made. Someone may want information, recommendations, independent choice, or delegation. A person who prefers an expert or AI to decide is not necessarily surrendering agency: delegation can express it (section 5.3, pp. 6-7).

Sunstein argues that mandatory active choice can override this second-order preference and therefore be coercively paternalistic. He also allows that a preference to delegate may sometimes be inferred rather than explicitly expressed. That introduces a practical problem for digital design: attributing consent to delegation requires evidence, especially where the provider benefits from passivity. This last design caution is the wiki's application of the argument, not an additional finding reported in the article.

## Evidence and policy-toolkit tensions

This is a conceptual and normative article drawing on existing research, not a new experiment. It reports no original data. Examples such as GPS navigation, retirement enrollment, safety warnings, and trans-fat restrictions illustrate the comparison; they do not establish one optimal policy across domains.

Nudges can complement mandates and bans by helping people comply. Interventions addressing harms to others are not paternalistic merely because they change behavior. Sunstein also cautions that an average effect across heterogeneous nudges tells little about which particular intervention works (pp. 4, 6).

There is a useful tension with the structural-reform discussion in [[Nudging and the Ethics of Nudging]]. Sunstein accepts that nudges might crowd out support for justified coercion but describes the evidence as weak (p. 4). That position should remain distinguishable from the stronger structural-policy concerns developed through [[Loewenstein and Chater, 2017|Putting Nudges in Perspective]].

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

The article explicitly includes websites, device defaults, and possible delegation to AI. Its broader value is to connect [[Ethics of Nudging]] with [[Chooser Welfare]]: evidence that users err must be paired with evidence that the architect can improve their decisions.

For the course, this supports comparing helpful defaults, competence-building, active choice, and delegation rather than treating more interaction as inherently more autonomous. It also supplies a bridge to [[Algorithmic Thought Partners]] and [[AI as Choice Aid]], while leaving an open institutional question: how can users distinguish assistance they would endorse from a provider's attempt to define their interests for them?
