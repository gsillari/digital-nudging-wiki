# Sunstein, 2023: Behavioral Biases, Choice Engines, and Paternalistic AI

## Summary

[[Sunstein, 2023|Behavioral Biases, Choice Engines, and Paternalistic AI]] asks how AI-powered "choice engines" might help consumers make better decisions when ordinary choice is distorted by limited information, present bias, inertia, limited attention, or other behavioral biases.

The paper starts from the problem of [[Chooser Welfare]] under heterogeneity. Average behavioral interventions can help some people and hurt others. Home energy reports, graphic labels, calorie labels, defaults, taxes, mandates, and bans may have different effects across groups. The fact that an intervention improves average behavior does not show that it improves each chooser's welfare.

Choice engines are Sunstein's constructive response. A user-facing AI tool could recommend products, plans, or defaults that fit a person's own situation, values, and constraints. It could offer simple rankings such as Good, Better, and Best; explain relevant tradeoffs; make future costs salient; and help users incorporate internalities and, where appropriate, externalities.

## Key claims

- Behavioral interventions are welfare-relevant only when they address real mistakes, not merely when they change behavior.
- Heterogeneity makes mass nudges, labels, taxes, mandates, and bans risky: the same intervention can help one group while burdening another.
- Present bias, limited attention, status quo bias, unrealistic optimism, and inadequate information can create internalities: costs people impose on their own future selves.
- AI choice engines might reduce internalities by helping people compare total costs, future consequences, and personal fit.
- Choice engines can range from nonpaternalistic menus to mildly, moderately, or highly paternalistic systems.
- The ethically attractive version preserves freedom of choice while making welfare-relevant information and recommendations easier to use.
- Choice engines can also be dangerous: they may be self-interested, manipulative, biased, coarse, or insufficiently informed about the user's actual preferences.
- Rules against dominated options, shrouded attributes, deception, and manipulation may be needed around AI choice aids.

## Evidence and examples

Sunstein uses several examples to show both promise and danger:

- Retirement-plan engines that automatically enroll employees or screen out unsuitable plans.
- Motor vehicle and appliance choice engines that make future fuel or energy savings salient.
- Good/Better/Best recommendation formats for users who want simplicity.
- "Keep It Simple" versus "Tell Me Everything" versions of an aid, depending on how much information a user wants.
- Possible "light patterns": friction such as "are you sure?" prompts or multiple clicks when the system predicts that a choice is harmful.
- Dominated retirement or consumer options, high-fee plans, and shrouded attributes as cases where choice engines need oversight.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This is a bridge source for [[AI as Choice Aid]]. It takes the classical nudge problem, "people often choose badly in complex environments," and asks what happens when the choice architect becomes an AI assistant rather than a static form, label, or default.

For the course, the important move is not that AI should replace choice. It is that AI can make the welfare standard more operational. Instead of inferring welfare from what people click, buy, or fail to change, a choice engine can help users compare consequences, surface hidden costs, and decide under better conditions.

The paper also preserves the ethical tension. A choice engine can be an [[Algorithmic Thought Partners|algorithmic thought partner]], but it can also become a personalized dark pattern if the builder optimizes for fees, conversion, data capture, or engagement.
