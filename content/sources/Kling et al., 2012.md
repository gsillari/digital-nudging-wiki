# Kling et al., 2012: Comparison Friction: Experimental Evidence from Medicare Drug Plans

## Summary

[[Kling et al., 2012|Comparison Friction]] names the wedge between the availability of comparative information and people's actual use of it. The paper studies Medicare Part D prescription drug plans, where personalized cost comparisons were technically available through Medicare's Plan Finder but many seniors did not access or use them.

In a randomized field experiment, an intervention group received a letter with personalized plan-cost information. A comparison group received general information about the website. The additional step of delivering personalized comparison information substantially changed behavior: plan switching rose from 17% to 28%, and predicted consumer costs fell by about $100 per year among letter recipients.

## Key claims

- Consumer choice and competition require not merely information availability but practical comparison.
- Comparison friction can be large even when information is free and formally easy to acquire.
- Personalized, delivered information can change choices more than passive access to a tool.
- Consumers may underestimate potential savings, which reduces the perceived value of looking.
- Small attention, expectation, and processing costs can block welfare-improving comparison.
- Policy designs that rely on consumer choice need to reduce comparison friction directly.

## Evidence and methods

The paper combines a randomized field experiment, survey evidence, audits of information sources, and cost calculations using Medicare Plan Finder data. The intervention letter listed annual cost information conditional on the person's drug profile, the lowest-cost plan, potential savings from switching, and the enrollment deadline.

The core result is simple and useful for teaching: a personalized letter increased switching by 11 percentage points and reduced predicted cost by about $100 per recipient, despite the fact that the information was already publicly available.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This is the canonical empirical source for comparison friction ([[Smart Disclosure]]). It shows why a digital choice aid must not merely host information somewhere. It must lower the effort of finding, processing, comparing, trusting, and acting on that information.

For [[AI as Choice Aid]], the lesson is direct. Good choice aids convert data availability into personalized sense-making. The important design variable is not simply how much information exists, but whether the user receives the comparison in a timely, comprehensible, action-guiding form.
