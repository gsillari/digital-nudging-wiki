# Bhargava et al., 2017: Choose to Lose: Health Plan Choices from a Menu with Dominated Options

## Summary

[[Bhargava et al., 2017|Choose to Lose]] studies health insurance choices in a setting where many options were financially dominated by otherwise equivalent high-deductible plans. Because the plans differed only in cost-sharing and premium, the case provides an unusually clean test of whether people make financially sensible insurance choices.

The main finding is stark: most employees chose dominated plans. These choices produced meaningful excess spending, were more common among low-income employees, and usually did not self-correct through later switching. The authors argue that the pattern is best explained by low insurance competence rather than by ordinary risk preferences, informed preference for low deductibles, or mere menu complexity.

## Key claims

- Revealed insurance choices can reflect misunderstanding rather than risk preferences.
- In the studied firm, 61% of employees chose nominally dominated plans and about 55% chose dominated plans after tax adjustment.
- Employees in dominated plans could have saved an average of about $372 per year by moving to the otherwise equivalent high-deductible alternative.
- Low-income employees were more likely to choose dominated plans.
- Subsequent plan switching reduced but did not eliminate dominated choice and widened some disparities.
- Hypothetical-choice experiments suggest that poor understanding of cost-sharing and plan value played a central role.
- Strong insurance competence was associated with near-elimination of dominated choices in the experimental evidence.

## Evidence and methods

The empirical setting is a large U.S. firm with 23,894 employees choosing among plans that shared the same insurer, services, and network but varied in deductible, copayment, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum, and premium.

The paper then uses hypothetical-choice experiments to distinguish explanations. Simplifying the menu did not eliminate dominated choice. Making dominance clear reduced dominated choices sharply. Measures of insurance understanding strongly predicted whether participants avoided dominated plans.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This paper gives the strongest welfare-warning in the choice-aid cluster. People can actively choose and still choose options that no plausible risk preference can justify. In such contexts, observed choice is poor evidence of [[Chooser Welfare]].

For [[AI as Choice Aid]], the lesson is not simply "recommend the cheapest plan." The better lesson is that choice aids must build competence: explain deductibles, premiums, worst-case exposure, dominated options, and tradeoffs in a way users can actually use.

The paper also matters for equity. If misunderstanding is socially patterned, then digital choice architecture that assumes competence can widen welfare losses for lower-income users.
