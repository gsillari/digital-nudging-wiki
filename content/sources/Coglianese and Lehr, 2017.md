# Coglianese and Lehr, 2017: Regulating by Robot

## Summary

[[Coglianese and Lehr, 2017|Regulating by Robot]] is the legal-defense foundation for algorithmic administration. Coglianese and Lehr ask whether machine-learning tools used for adjudication or rulemaking can fit within constitutional and administrative-law doctrines such as nondelegation, due process, equal protection, reason-giving, and transparency.

Their answer is broadly yes. Properly understood, machine-learning systems need not be unaccountable computerized overlords. They can be treated as extensions of ordinary administrative tools: statistical models, decision aids, instruments, and delegated decision procedures that remain governed by humanly specified objectives, validation, and legal constraints.

## Key claims

- Administrative agencies can use machine learning in adjudication and rulemaking without necessarily violating constitutional or administrative law.
- Nondelegation concerns can be addressed when algorithms are tied to an intelligible principle: an objective function, statutory goal, or policy criterion specified by humans.
- Due process analysis should be pragmatic, especially under balancing frameworks such as Mathews v. Eldridge.
- Transparency and reason-giving do not require full intuitive access to every internal computation if agencies can explain the system's objective, data, validation, and role in the decision.
- Equal protection concerns depend on how models are designed, trained, validated, and used, not simply on the fact that they are algorithmic.
- Algorithmic tools may reduce some forms of arbitrary human discretion, but they create new risks around opacity, validation, and institutional responsibility.

## Legal doctrines unpacked

- Nondelegation: the concern is that public authority might be shifted from legally accountable officials to an algorithmic system, vendor, or technical process. Coglianese and Lehr respond that machine-learning systems can satisfy the relevant concern when humans specify the intelligible principle: the statutory goal, policy criterion, prediction target, or objective function that guides the system.
- Mathews v. Eldridge: the due-process framework asks how much procedure is required by balancing the affected private interest, the risk of erroneous deprivation and value of additional safeguards, and the government's administrative interest. For algorithmic decision-making, this makes due process a question of stakes, error, safeguards, and institutional role rather than a categorical ban on machine learning.
- Equal protection: the question is not whether the decision-maker is algorithmic, but whether the system's data, features, labels, validation, thresholds, deployment, or human reliance create legally relevant unequal treatment or unjustified group burdens. This makes the paper a bridge to fairness metrics ([[Algorithmic Fairness]]) and sociotechnical fairness ([[Algorithmic Fairness]]) without reducing equal protection to any one statistical criterion.
- Transparency and reason-giving: agencies need not provide full intuitive access to every internal computation. They must be able to explain the system's objective, evidence base, validation, limits, and decision role well enough to support accountability and contestation.

## Course use

Use this paper before the more recent management-based-regulation papers. It establishes the first step in the argument: algorithmic government is not legally impossible. Once that defense is on the table, the course can move to the design question: what kinds of governance systems make algorithmic administration legitimate?

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

Digital nudging often operates in the borderland between recommendation and decision. This paper helps explain why the institutional setting matters. A predictive score, ranking, or automated suggestion may be lawful and useful, but only if its objective, validation, contestability, and human role can be justified.

For the course, the paper helps connect algorithmic nudging to administrative governance: the problem is not simply whether a system uses AI, but whether the institutional use of AI is legally and publicly accountable.
