# Mills, 2022: Personalized Nudging (Mills)

## Summary

[[Mills, 2022|Personalized Nudging (Mills)]] gives a systematic framework for understanding what gets personalized in a personalized nudge. Its core contribution is a two-component distinction: choice personalization and delivery personalization.

For this vault, "choice personalization" is also recorded as content or target personalization, because it concerns what the person is nudged toward. "Delivery personalization" concerns how the nudge is delivered or which nudge mechanism is selected.

## Key claims

- Impersonal nudges can fail because populations are heterogeneous. A nudge that benefits many people may hurt or misfit people with different circumstances.
- Personalization is a response to heterogeneity, not merely a synonym for Big Data.
- Choice personalization uses heterogeneity data to personalize the outcome or target of the nudge when the nudge mechanism is already selected.
- Delivery personalization uses heterogeneity data to personalize the method of nudging, such as selecting a default, social-norm message, reminder, or other nudge type.
- The two forms can be combined, as when a system personalizes both the medium of an intervention and the product, action, or option promoted through that medium.
- Personalized nudging does not necessarily require Big Data. Some forms can be built with relatively simple data such as birth dates or other temporal landmarks.
- More detailed data can expand personalization opportunities, but this increases privacy, access, governance, and accountability burdens.
- Personalized nudging can undermine universality, shared social experiences, transparency, and public scrutiny, especially when people receive different regulatory or behavioral interventions.

## Two-component framework

- Choice or content personalization: personalization within nudges. The architect has selected the nudge type and uses data to determine the best target or outcome. Mills's example is a savings nudge that uses a personally meaningful "fresh start" date or chooses a different default contribution level for different people.
- Delivery personalization: personalization across nudges. The architect uses data to determine which nudge mechanism is most effective for a person. One person may respond to a default; another may respond better to a social-norm message, reminder, or information format.

This distinction is the paper's durable value for the course. It lets us separate personalization of mechanism from personalization of target.

## Relationship to Behavioral Economics 2.0

Mills is conceptually related to [[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]], but less radical. Mills systematizes personalization within the nudge paradigm: the architect can personalize what is nudged toward or how the nudge is delivered.

Ludwig et al. push further. Their Behavioral Economics 2.0 ([[Algorithmic Thought Partners]]) asks whether algorithms can help determine what direction is right for the person under reflective equilibrium ([[Algorithmic Thought Partners]]). Mills therefore complements the programmatic paper by supplying a cleaner vocabulary for the levels at which personalization can occur.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This paper helps prevent a common ambiguity in [[Digital Nudging]]. When a platform says that a nudge is personalized, it may mean at least two different things:

- The system personalized the delivery mechanism, such as timing, channel, frame, salience, or nudge type.
- The system personalized the target, such as which option, default, product, contribution rate, or behavior the user is nudged toward.

Those are ethically and technically different. Delivery personalization can improve effectiveness without changing the behavioral target. Content or target personalization can change the welfare claim itself, because the system is asserting that a different outcome is better for this particular person.

The paper also sits between [[Hypernudge]] and precision nudging ([[Personalized Choice Architecture]]). Like Yeung, Mills treats data-driven personalization as powerful and governance-sensitive. Unlike Yeung, the paper is not primarily a critique of structural harms. It offers a practical conceptual framework for distinguishing kinds of personalization, while still warning about universality, data access, and privacy.
