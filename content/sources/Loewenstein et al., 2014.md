# Loewenstein et al., 2014: Disclosure: Psychology Changes Everything

## Summary

[[Loewenstein et al., 2014|Disclosure: Psychology Changes Everything]] reviews mandatory disclosure as a policy tool and argues that psychology radically complicates the standard economic case for it. Disclosure is attractive because it seems choice-preserving: rather than banning, mandating, or taxing, the regulator provides information and lets people decide.

The problem is that information availability is not the same as information use. Limited attention, motivated attention, biased probability judgments, complexity, delay, and disclosure overload can make disclosure ineffective or even counterproductive. Disclosures can also change provider behavior, sometimes beneficially through a "telltale heart" effect and sometimes perversely through moral licensing.

## Key claims

- Disclosure is most plausible where incentives are misaligned and one party has information that the other lacks.
- Standard economic accounts overestimate disclosure when they assume that recipients notice, understand, believe, and use the information.
- Limited attention makes many disclosures practically invisible, especially when they are long, technical, numerous, or poorly timed.
- Motivated attention means people may avoid, discount, or fail to update from unwelcome information.
- Disclosure of conflicts of interest can backfire if advisors feel morally licensed or if advisees feel social pressure to follow now-distrusted advice.
- Disclosure can still work when it changes provider behavior, not only when it changes consumer behavior.
- Better disclosure is simplified, standardized, comparative, vivid where appropriate, and often mediated by intermediaries or tools.

## Evidence and methods

The article is a review, not a single empirical study. It synthesizes work on product risk warnings, financial disclosures, calorie and nutrition labeling, restaurant hygiene grades, conflict-of-interest disclosure, mutual fund disclosure, social comparison information, and smart-disclosure initiatives.

The review's practical section is especially important for digital nudging. It highlights simplification, standardized and comparative information, social comparison information, vividness, smart disclosure, intermediaries, and carefully designed in-person disclosure.

## Why it matters for Digital Nudging

This source explains why "just disclose it" is rarely enough. Digital environments can make information technically available while keeping it practically unusable through length, timing, visual hierarchy, jargon, or distraction.

For [[AI as Choice Aid]], the paper supplies the design premise: the goal is not more information in the abstract, but usable sense-making. AI tools can help only if they reduce the psychological costs of understanding, comparing, and acting on information. Otherwise they risk becoming another layer of disclosure theater.

The paper also connects to [[Smart Disclosure]]. Machine-readable data and intermediaries can make disclosure useful when direct human processing is unrealistic, but the intermediary itself becomes a new choice architect whose incentives must be scrutinized.
