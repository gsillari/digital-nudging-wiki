# Sociotechnical Fairness

## Core idea

[[Sociotechnical Fairness]] is the view that fairness is a property of a whole decision-making system, not a mathematical property of a predictive algorithm by itself. The relevant system includes the data, model, decision rule, threshold, interface, human discretion, organizational incentives, legal context, appeal procedures, and social consequences.

For the course, this is the endpoint of the first fairness branch. It preserves [[Fairness Metrics]] as useful diagnostics while rejecting the idea that satisfying a metric makes a digital or institutional system fair.

## Key distinctions

- Algorithm versus decision system: a model may score, rank, or predict, but fairness depends on how that output is used.
- Metric as diagnostic versus metric as objective: parity failures can reveal possible harm, but parity satisfaction does not certify justice.
- Discrimination versus justice: a system can avoid one measured disparity while remaining invalid, punitive, exploitative, demeaning, opaque, or badly governed.
- Technical subsystem versus algorithmic bureaucracy: automated decision support belongs to a larger administrative process with goals, discretion, accountability, and politics.
- Bias audit versus system audit: checking group disparities is only one part of evaluating validity, purpose, contestability, human oversight, and downstream consequences.
- Replacement versus complementarity: fairness can depend on whether an algorithm replaces human judgment, assists it, disciplines it, or creates new forms of discretion.

## Evidence and debate

[[Narayanan, 2026|What If Algorithmic Fairness Is a Category Error?]] supplies the central argument. Narayanan treats algorithmic fairness metrics as a narrow slice of a broader accountability problem. He uses COMPAS, hiring automation, social services, and algorithmic bureaucracies to show why model-level fairness can miss the more important design questions.

[[Kleinberg et al., 2018|Human Decisions and Machine Predictions]] and [[Ludwig and Mullainathan, 2021|Fragile Algorithms and Fallible Decision-Makers]] add the efficiency side of the sociotechnical point. Whether an algorithm improves decisions depends on how predictions, payoffs, counterfactuals, and human overrides are organized. Efficiency, like fairness, is a property of the full decision system.

This does not erase the earlier sequence. [[Predictive Parity]] can still matter because scores should have consistent evidential meaning. [[Error Rate Parity]] can still matter because mistakes impose practical burdens. [[Algorithmic Fairness as Political Philosophy]] can still matter because metrics encode theories of justice. [[Fairness as Policy Alignment]] can still matter because metrics interact with policy goals. Sociotechnical fairness says that all of these remain incomplete unless the full system is examined.

## Practical or policy relevance

For [[Digital Nudging]], sociotechnical fairness is crucial because influence often happens through soft steering rather than formal denial. A recommender system, feed, personalization engine, search ranking, consent flow, fraud screen, or AI assistant may shape opportunity and behavior through exposure, salience, friction, timing, explanation, and escalation.

A sociotechnical audit should ask what the system optimizes, whose welfare counts, how personalization changes over time, what users and regulators can see, whether people can contest outputs, how human reviewers interact with the model, and whether the system should exist in its current institutional role.
