# Algorithmic Governance

## Core idea

[[Algorithmic Governance]] names the legal and institutional framework for using algorithms in public administration and other high-stakes decision systems. It asks when algorithmic tools may lawfully and legitimately predict, rank, recommend, adjudicate, triage, explain, or automate.

This page now absorbs the former pages on the automated state, AI heterogeneity and multifunctionality, management-based AI regulation, human oversight, and human-guided training. They are best taught as parts of one governance sequence.

## Legal compatibility

The first governance question is whether algorithmic adjudication and rulemaking can fit existing administrative law. The answer is yes only if public institutions retain responsibility for objectives, validation, reasons, due process, and contestability. The algorithm is not an independent sovereign; it is an instrument inside a legal institution. Coglianese and Lehr develop this legal-compatibility argument in [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2017|Regulating by Robot]].

The key doctrines are nondelegation, due process, equal protection, transparency, and reason-giving. Nondelegation concerns are softened when humans specify the intelligible principle: the statutory goal, prediction target, or policy criterion. Due process is analyzed pragmatically through the Mathews v. Eldridge balance: private interest, risk of error and value of safeguards, and government burden.

Transparency does not require full intuitive access to every internal computation. The more important administrative-law demand is reasoned transparency: the agency must explain the system's objective, data, validation, limits, and role in the decision. Coglianese and Lehr develop that distinction in [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2019|Transparency and Algorithmic Governance]].

## Expertise, noise, and empathy

The affirmative case for algorithmic administration is not only technical efficiency. Human systems are noisy and cognitively biased; similarly situated people may receive different outcomes because of the official, the sequence of prior cases, or salient but overweighted features. Algorithms can reduce some of that noise and bias in prediction problems. Sunstein gives this behavioral-law case in [[Sunstein, 2022|Governing by Algorithm? No Noise and (Potentially) Less Bias]].

The legitimacy condition is that expertise must not become administrative coldness. An automated state may improve expertise and accountability, but it must remain empathic: able to hear people, explain itself, escalate unusual cases, and preserve human responsiveness when rights, dignity, welfare, or opportunity are at stake. Coglianese makes this the central caution in [[Coglianese, 2021|Administrative Law in the Automated State]].

## Heterogeneity and multifunctionality

The later governance question is how to regulate systems whose risks vary by use, design, data, objective, user, domain, institutional setting, and decision role. Foundation models intensify this heterogeneity because one system can become a tutor, sales agent, recommender, bureaucratic chatbot, eligibility screen, or behavioral coach. Coglianese and Crum shift the argument from legal defense to governance design across [[Coglianese and Crum, 2024|Taking Training Seriously]], [[Coglianese and Crum, 2025a|Leashes, Not Guardrails]], and [[Coglianese and Crum, 2025b|Regulating Multifunctionality]].

This is the multifunctionality problem. The risk is not only in the model family; it is in the combination of model, use, user, interface, goal, and institution. A one-size-fits-all rule will often underreach, overreach, or become obsolete.

## Management and oversight

Management-based regulation is the governance response. Rather than prescribing every permitted output or banned design feature in advance, law can require organizations to maintain internal systems for identifying, assessing, mitigating, documenting, auditing, and revising risks.

Human oversight belongs across the lifecycle, not only after deployment. Human-guided training can shape what the system learns, what counts as success, which errors matter, what explanations it can support, and which domain values are treated as salient. Oversight after deployment still matters: auditing, contestability, escalation, documentation, monitoring, and revision keep the leash in human hands.

## Digital relevance

Digital nudging becomes a governance problem when ranking models, recommender systems, defaults, prompts, experiments, or AI assistants generate choice architecture. The question is no longer only whether one visible nudge is transparent or welfare-promoting. It is whether the system that produces nudges has legitimate objectives, validation evidence, reasoned explanations, fairness monitoring, human oversight, and meaningful contestation.
