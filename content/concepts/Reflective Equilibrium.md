# Reflective Equilibrium

## Core idea

[[Reflective Equilibrium]] is used in [[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]] as the target for algorithmic behavioral support: the decision a person would make if they exercised their most considered judgment on the information feasibly available at the time.

In this vault, reflective equilibrium is closely related to [[Chooser Welfare]]. It gives a more operational form to the idea that a nudge or tool should help people by their own lights.

## Key distinctions

- Reflective equilibrium vs revealed preference: actual behavior may reflect confusion, missing information, bias, friction, or pressure.
- Reflective equilibrium vs omniscience: the target is not perfect foresight; it is the best decision available under feasible information at the time.
- Outcome vs process: the goal is the equivalent of considered judgment, not necessarily long deliberation in every case.
- User direction vs intervention direction: the standard asks what direction is right for this person, not how to move them toward a preset direction.
- Target personalization vs reflective equilibrium: a nudge can personalize its target without proving that the target matches the user's considered judgment.

## Digital relevance

Reflective equilibrium offers a welfare standard for digital nudging, but it also creates a hard measurement problem. If an algorithm changes choices, the course still needs to ask whether those choices are closer to the user's considered judgment.

Possible evidence includes decision stability under new frames, responses to additional information, explicit user endorsement after explanation, long-term satisfaction, and behavioral models of known biases. None is sufficient alone.

Mills's content-personalization category is a useful stepping stone toward this standard. It shows where the target can vary by user, while Ludwig et al. ask how the system could know which target is right.
