# Transparency and Publicity

## Core idea

[[Transparency and Publicity]] are safeguards for behaviorally informed influence. Transparency asks whether the relevant action, reason, and effect can be understood by affected people. Publicity asks whether the choice architecture can withstand public scrutiny.

In [[Sunstein, 2016|The Ethics of Influence: Government in the Age of Behavioral Science]], transparency is central to ethical nudging, but it is not a magic solvent. A transparent nudge may still be objectionable if it manipulates, humiliates, or serves an illicit end.

[[Hansen and Jespersen, 2013|Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice]] distinguishes publicity from epistemic transparency. A policy may be publicly defensible in principle while the target still cannot reconstruct the intention or mechanism in the moment of influence.

[[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge: Big Data as a Mode of Regulation by Design]] makes the digital transparency problem sharper. A platform may disclose that it personalizes or improves services while keeping the operative ranking model, data flows, A/B tests, and optimization target opaque.

[[Coglianese and Lehr, 2019|Transparency and Algorithmic Governance]] adds a legal distinction that is useful for digital nudging: fishbowl transparency and [[Reasoned Transparency]]. Fishbowl transparency concerns access to information such as records, code, data, and logs. Reasoned transparency concerns the why of a decision: objective, justification, validation, and decision role. Full code access is not always necessary or sufficient, but a system still needs an intelligible account of what it is trying to do and why it is legitimate.

## Key distinctions

- Visible design vs hidden design: users may see an interface element without understanding the behavioral objective behind it.
- Disclosure vs usable transparency: a long notice may disclose information without making it practically available for decision-making.
- Publicity vs private intelligibility: a design may be understandable to an individual but still lack institutional accountability.
- Transparency of content vs transparency of mechanism: a warning may be visible, while the reason for its timing, ranking, or personalization remains hidden.
- Epistemic transparency vs disclosure: an intervention is epistemically transparent when the target can reasonably recognize the intention and means of influence, not merely when a disclosure exists somewhere.
- Algorithmic transparency vs user-facing transparency: a user may need simple explanations, while researchers or regulators may need deeper access to data, models, experiments, and audit trails.
- Fishbowl transparency vs reasoned transparency: public access to information is different from an adequate explanation of objectives, validation, and reasons.
- Transparency vs auditability: a system may not be fully interpretable to every user, but it should preserve records and evidence that allow competent review.

## Digital relevance

Digital nudging makes transparency difficult because the relevant choice architecture may be dynamic, personalized, experimental, or algorithmic. Users may not know which options were hidden, which ranking rule applied, why a default was selected, or what objective the system optimized.

Proprietary systems add another layer. Firms may resist disclosure by invoking trade secrecy, security, competitive advantage, or product complexity. The course should therefore treat transparency as a layered requirement: user-facing clarity, researcher or regulator access, auditability, and institutional accountability may each do different work.

For hypernudges, transparency must also be temporal. A system that changes continuously needs records of what changed, who was exposed, what metric was optimized, and what effects were observed.

[[Mills, 2022|Personalized Nudging (Mills)]] adds that personalization can undermine universality, which often helps make laws, regulations, and nudges publicly comparable. If different people receive different defaults, messages, or targets, the basis of the difference must be visible enough for scrutiny.

The administrative-law papers add that opacity is not a binary. A proprietary or technically complex system can still be required to explain its purpose, validation, evidence, and governance process. For digital nudging, this supports a layered transparency model: users need intelligible reasons, auditors need access to evidence, and regulators need enough fishbowl access to test whether the reasoned account is true.
