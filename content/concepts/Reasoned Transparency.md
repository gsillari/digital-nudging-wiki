# Reasoned Transparency

## Core idea

[[Reasoned Transparency]] is transparency about why a decision or system is justified. It differs from fishbowl transparency, which is transparency about what information, data, code, records, or activities can be inspected.

In [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2019|Transparency and Algorithmic Governance]], Coglianese and Lehr argue that legal transparency does not always require complete access to every internal computation. It often requires an adequate public reason: the objective being pursued, the evidence used, the design choices made, the validation performed, and the role of the system in the decision.

## Fishbowl vs reasoned transparency

- Fishbowl transparency: access to documents, data, code, meetings, logs, or records.
- Reasoned transparency: an intelligible account of purpose, justification, design, validation, and decision role.
- Fishbowl transparency can support reasoned transparency, but raw access is not always enough.
- Reasoned transparency can sometimes be possible even when full model internals are technically complex or proprietary.

## Digital relevance

Digital nudging needs both forms. Users may need a simple reason why a recommendation, default, warning, price, ranking, or friction appeared. Regulators may need fishbowl access to experiments, objectives, data flows, and audit trails. The public may need a reasoned explanation of why the system's behavioral influence is legitimate.

This distinction is crucial for proprietary systems. A platform might refuse to disclose source code, but it should still be able to explain what the system optimizes, how it was tested, what risks it monitors, and how affected people can contest outcomes.
