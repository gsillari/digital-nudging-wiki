# Smart Disclosure

## Core idea

[[Smart Disclosure]] is a strategy for turning formally available information into usable comparison infrastructure. The problem is not simply that users lack information. It is that complex choices often require standardized product data, access to one's own usage history, and software-mediated comparison before disclosure can actually improve decisions.

The basic move is to release complex information in standardized, machine-readable form so that choice engines, apps, intermediaries, or AI tools can search, filter, compare, and recommend. In [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]], this makes disclosure part of [[Choice Architecture]]: the goal is not more text, but better mappings between options and welfare.

## Key distinctions

- Formal disclosure vs usable disclosure: information can be disclosed yet remain practically unreadable.
- Human-readable vs machine-readable: machine-readable disclosure allows decision tools to compare complex products and services.
- Product data vs usage data: smart disclosure requires both standardized product attributes and user access to their own histories.
- Choice engines vs static labels: choice engines use data to search, filter, compare, and recommend.
- Transparency vs steering: smart disclosure can empower users, but the tools built on it need governance because rankings and recommendations can be self-serving.

## Evidence and debate

Smart disclosure starts from the failure of ordinary disclosure. Information can be technically available while remaining practically useless because users miss it, distrust it, lack motivation to process it, or cannot compare it across options. Loewenstein and coauthors make this psychological case in [[Loewenstein et al., 2014|Disclosure: Psychology Changes Everything]].

The empirical mechanism is comparison friction ([[Smart Disclosure]]): the wedge between available comparative information and actual comparison behavior. Kling and coauthors show this in the Medicare Part D setting, where personalized cost information changed plan switching and reduced costs even though the relevant information was already formally available in [[Kling et al., 2012|Comparison Friction]].

The smart-disclosure response is data infrastructure: product attributes and personal usage data must be standardized, portable, timely, and machine-readable enough for tools to make comparison feasible. [[Nudge - Chapter 7 Smart Disclosure]] develops this through phone plans, fuel economy, APR standardization, online terms, travel websites, Open Banking, energy-choice apps, grocery allergens, and platform usage histories.

The main debate is whether this infrastructure empowers users or merely relocates influence to new intermediaries. Choice engines and recommendation systems can be user-serving, but they can also become [[Hypernudge|hypernudges]] if ranking, salience, and selection are optimized for the architect's interests. Yeung's [[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge: Big Data as a Mode of Regulation by Design]] gives the warning.

The AI version goes beyond comparison toward sense-making. Algorithmic tools can translate complex terms, surface shrouded attributes, flag hidden costs, and help users reason through consequences. This makes smart disclosure part of adaptive armor ([[Algorithmic Thought Partners]]), not only data release; Ludwig and coauthors develop that constructive direction in [[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]].

## Practical or policy relevance

Smart disclosure is highly relevant to digital nudging because many digital choices are too complex for static labels. Machine-readable data can support comparison tools for banking, energy, subscriptions, insurance, health plans, app privacy, algorithmic recommendations, and platform terms.

The governance question is whether the data infrastructure empowers the user or simply supplies better inputs for personalized steering. Smart disclosure needs rules for data quality, standardization, portability, security, tool incentives, ranking logic, explanation, and contestability.

For [[AI as Choice Aid]], smart disclosure is infrastructure rather than a complete solution. The user-facing layer still has to make the data understandable, balanced, trustworthy, and action-guiding.
