# Smart Disclosure

## Core idea

[[Smart Disclosure]] is a disclosure strategy in which complex information is released in standardized, machine-readable form and users can access relevant data about their own past usage or purchases. In [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]], it is meant to make disclosure useful for software-mediated comparison rather than merely legally available.

## Key distinctions

- Formal disclosure vs usable disclosure: information can be disclosed yet remain practically unreadable.
- Human-readable vs machine-readable: machine-readable disclosure allows decision tools to compare complex products and services.
- Product data vs usage data: smart disclosure requires both standardized product attributes and user access to their own histories.
- Choice engines vs static labels: choice engines use data to search, filter, compare, and recommend.
- Transparency vs steering: smart disclosure can empower users, but the tools built on it need governance because rankings and recommendations can be self-serving.

## Evidence and debate

[[Nudge - Chapter 7 Smart Disclosure]] develops the concept through phone plans, fuel economy, APR standardization, online terms, travel websites, Open Banking, energy-choice apps, grocery allergens, and platform usage histories. It builds on [[Nudge - Chapter 5 Choice Architecture]] by treating data infrastructure as a way to improve mappings and structure complex choices.

The key debate is whether data access and choice engines actually empower users or simply shift influence to intermediaries whose incentives may be opaque.

[[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge: Big Data as a Mode of Regulation by Design]] sharpens this debate. Choice engines and recommendation systems can act as user-serving decision aids, but they can also become hypernudges that optimize ranking, salience, and selection for the architect's interests.

## Practical or policy relevance

Smart disclosure is highly relevant to digital nudging because many digital choices are too complex for static labels. Machine-readable data can support comparison tools for banking, energy, subscriptions, insurance, health plans, app privacy, algorithmic recommendations, and platform terms.

The governance question is whether the data infrastructure empowers the user or simply supplies better inputs for personalized steering.

[[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]] adds a more active model: algorithmic tools can translate complex terms, surface shrouded attributes, and help users reason through consequences. This makes smart disclosure part of [[Adaptive Armor]], not only data release.
