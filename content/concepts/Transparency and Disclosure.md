# Transparency and Disclosure

## Core idea

[[Transparency and Disclosure]] collects the course's vocabulary for making choice architecture visible, intelligible, contestable, and publicly accountable. Transparency asks whether affected people and publics can understand the action, reason, and relevant effects of an intervention. Disclosure is one instrument for transparency, but disclosure can fail when information is too long, complex, badly timed, or strategically buried.

This page absorbs the former page on transparency and publicity and the former page on reasoned transparency. The distinction matters: ethical digital nudging needs both user-facing clarity and institutional reason-giving.

## Publicity, epistemic transparency, and disclosure

Publicity is an ethical test, not merely a notice requirement. A nudge should be visible enough, publicly defensible, and compatible with autonomy, dignity, welfare, and self-government. Sunstein's [[Sunstein, 2016|The Ethics of Influence: Government in the Age of Behavioral Science]] supplies this publicity standard.

Epistemic transparency is narrower and more immediate: can the target recognize the intervention and its purpose while choosing? A nudge may be publicly announced while the target still cannot recognize its intention or mechanism in context. Hansen and Jespersen's [[Hansen and Jespersen, 2013|Nudge and the Manipulation of Choice]] is the anchor for this distinction between public defensibility and transparency at the point of influence.

Transparency also matters because it controls power. In Schmidt and Engelen's [[Schmidt and Engelen, 2020|overview]], the non-domination worry is that nudging can give architects uncontrolled influence over citizens or users. The response is not mere notice, but transparency plus democratic control, accountability, and contestability.

Disclosure is weaker still. A cookie notice, terms page, or long explanation may disclose information without making it practically usable. The course should therefore ask whether disclosure becomes understanding, and whether understanding arrives at the right time.

## Fishbowl and reasoned transparency

Algorithmic governance needs a second distinction: access to institutional materials is not the same as an explanation of reasons. Fishbowl transparency is access to records, code, data, logs, documents, meetings, and other evidence of what an institution did. Reasoned transparency is the why: the objective, justification, design choice, validation, limits, and role of a system in the decision. Coglianese and Lehr develop this distinction in [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2019|Transparency and Algorithmic Governance]].

Digital nudging needs both. Users may need a simple reason why a recommendation, default, warning, price, ranking, or friction appeared. Regulators may need fishbowl access to experiments, objectives, data flows, and audit trails. The public may need a reasoned explanation of why the system's behavioral influence is legitimate.

The digital-nudging application is the vault's course interpretation of Coglianese and Lehr's administrative-law distinction.

## Digital relevance

Digital nudging makes transparency difficult because the relevant choice architecture may be dynamic, personalized, experimental, proprietary, or algorithmic. A user may see the interface while not knowing which options were hidden, which ranking rule applied, why a default was selected, or what objective was optimized.

For hypernudges, transparency must also be temporal. A system that changes continuously needs records of what changed, who was exposed, what metric was optimized, and what effects were observed.

Yeung's [[Yeung, 2017|Hypernudge: Big Data as a Mode of Regulation by Design]] is the anchor for that problem: data-driven choice architecture can be personalized and continuously modified while remaining difficult for users and outsiders to reconstruct.
