# Behavioral Economics

## Core idea

[[Behavioral Economics]] studies how real people make judgments and decisions when attention, information, cognitive capacity, emotion, self-control, social influence, and institutional design depart from the assumptions of the Econ model. It gives the course its mechanism vocabulary: people use shortcuts, follow defaults, respond to frames, procrastinate, misread risk, care about social cues, and often decide under conditions that make full optimization unrealistic.

The canonical course contrast is between Econs and Humans. Econs optimize under idealized assumptions; Humans are fallible, situated, and context-sensitive. Thaler and Sunstein make this contrast the foundation for [[Nudge|nudging]] in [[Thaler and Sunstein, 2021|Nudge: The Final Edition]].

## Key distinctions

- Econ vs Human: Econs optimize under standard assumptions; Humans use shortcuts, forget, follow defaults, respond to frames, and care about social cues.
- Fallibility vs irrationality: Thaler and Sunstein avoid treating people as foolish. Their claim is that people are fallible and environments can make decisions hard.
- Judgment biases vs self-control problems: anchoring, availability, representativeness, loss aversion, and framing differ from hot-cold states, temptation, and Planner/Doer conflict.
- Individual cognition vs social influence: people are shaped both by internal shortcuts and by what others appear to think or do.
- Education vs design: boosting competence can help, but the book argues that good choice architecture remains necessary because many decisions are too complex, rare, or poorly timed for education alone.

## Evidence and debate

The classical foundation is a set of mechanisms: heuristics, biases, framing, automatic and reflective cognition, self-control problems, temptation, commitment devices, mental accounting, conformity, social norms, cascades, identity, and pluralistic ignorance. [[Nudge - Part I Humans and Econs]] maps these mechanisms for the course.

The applied layer converts mechanisms into design questions. Interventions can reduce effort, increase salience, use social context, and reach people when action is possible, but they still need contextual testing. [[EAST Framework]] is the compact practitioner version of that applied layer.

The policy layer keeps behavioral economics broader than nudging. Behavioral analysis can diagnose why a policy problem arises, how people and firms respond to traditional tools, and how taxes, mandates, subsidies, or disclosures should be designed. Sometimes the best response to a behavioral problem is not a nudge but a stronger policy instrument; this is the warning in Loewenstein and Chater's [[Loewenstein and Chater, 2017|Putting Nudges in Perspective]].

The digital layer asks whether algorithms can do more than exploit predictable mistakes. If behavioral economics identifies where choice goes wrong, algorithmic behavioral economics asks whether tools can help people correct mistakes while respecting heterogeneous preferences. Ludwig and coauthors develop this as [[Ludwig et al., 2025|Algorithms as a Vehicle to Reflective Equilibrium: Behavioral Economics 2.0]].

The debate for the course is not whether every bias always appears. It is whether a digital environment activates predictable vulnerabilities, and whether design can responsibly reduce error without becoming manipulative or paternalistic in an objectionable way.

## Practical or policy relevance

Digital environments are rich behavioral environments. They can anchor users with suggested values, exploit inertia through autoplay or renewal, make social proof salient, hide costs, overwhelm attention, or help users by simplifying and timing decisions better. Behavioral economics gives the course its mechanism vocabulary.

Behavioral Economics 2.0 ([[Algorithmic Thought Partners]]) adds a tool-building vocabulary: algorithms can explain, predict, warn, implement, protect, and train, not only nudge.
