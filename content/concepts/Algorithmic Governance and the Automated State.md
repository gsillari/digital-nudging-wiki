# Algorithmic Governance and the Automated State

## Core idea

[[Algorithmic Governance and the Automated State]] names the legal and institutional framework for using algorithms in public administration and other high-stakes governance systems. The cluster moves in two stages.

The early Coglianese-Lehr papers defend legal compatibility. Machine-learning tools can fit within administrative law when agencies specify objectives, validate systems, provide reasons, preserve due process, and retain responsibility. The later Coglianese-Crum papers move from legal defense to governance design: because AI is heterogeneous, multifunctional, and dynamic, regulation should rely heavily on management-based systems, human oversight, and lifecycle risk management.

## Development of the argument

- Legal defense: [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2017|Regulating by Robot]] argues that algorithmic adjudication and rulemaking can fit nondelegation, due process, equal protection, transparency, and reason-giving doctrines.
- Transparency refinement: [[Coglianese and Lehr, 2019|Transparency and Algorithmic Governance]] distinguishes fishbowl transparency from [[Reasoned Transparency]] and argues that black-box systems are not automatically barred if objectives, validation, and reasons can be explained.
- Normative endpoint: [[Coglianese, 2021|Administrative Law in the Automated State]] argues that automation may advance expertise and accountability, but an automated state must remain empathic.
- Governance design: [[Coglianese and Crum, 2024|Taking Training Seriously]], [[Coglianese and Crum, 2025b|Regulating Multifunctionality]], and [[Coglianese and Crum, 2025a|Leashes, Not Guardrails]] argue that modern AI requires management-based regulation, human-guided training, and flexible leashes rather than static guardrails.

## Digital relevance

Digital nudging sits inside algorithmic governance whenever platforms, agencies, or AI systems steer attention, salience, ranking, defaults, or decision support. The governance problem is not only whether a nudge is individually transparent or welfare-promoting. It is whether the system that produces nudges has legitimate objectives, validation evidence, human oversight, reason-giving, contestability, and empathy.

For the course, this concept becomes the administrative-law companion to the fairness and hypernudging modules. It says that algorithmic choice architecture can be legally compatible and socially useful, but only when embedded in accountable institutions.
