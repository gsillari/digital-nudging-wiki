# AI Heterogeneity and Multifunctionality

## Core idea

[[AI Heterogeneity and Multifunctionality]] names the reason one-size-fits-all AI regulation often fails. AI is not a single technology with one stable risk profile. Systems vary by design, data, objective, user, domain, institutional setting, and decision role.

Foundation models and generative AI deepen the problem because the same model can perform many functions. A general-purpose model can become a tutor, medical assistant, sales agent, search interface, coding assistant, bureaucratic chatbot, content recommender, or behavioral coach. The risk is therefore not only in the model; it is in the combination of model, use, user, goal, and institution.

## Key distinctions

- Use heterogeneity: the same system can be used for different tasks and harms.
- Design heterogeneity: architectures, training data, objectives, and interfaces vary widely.
- Problem heterogeneity: some domains have clear labels and low stakes, while others involve ambiguous values, scarce feedback, or severe consequences.
- Multifunctionality: foundation models can support many downstream functions from the same underlying model.
- Dynamic heterogeneity: systems change through updates, fine-tuning, feedback loops, and new deployment contexts.

## Digital relevance

Digital nudging depends on context. The same design feature can help, manipulate, simplify, obscure, inform, exploit, or protect depending on its target, timing, objective, and user vulnerability. AI multifunctionality makes this sharper: a generative assistant can nudge by explaining, ranking, warning, framing, discouraging, drafting, or silently filtering options.

For the course, this concept explains why governance must move from static taxonomies to lifecycle management, reason-giving, audits, and institutional accountability.
